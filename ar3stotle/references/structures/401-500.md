# Structures 401-500

## 401. Marriage of Convenience
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Practical reason for marriage established
2. Wedding without love
3. Cohabitation reveals each to the other
4. Genuine attraction develops
5. Misunderstanding or threat
6. Real love acknowledged; arrangement becomes real marriage
Description: Two characters marry for non-romantic reasons (citizenship, inheritance, scandal-prevention) and gradually fall in love within the practical arrangement. Wedding first, love after.
Exemplars: Helen Hoang's The Heart Principle (variant); many regency romances; The Proposal (Bullock); Sandra Brown novels.

## 402. Fake Dating
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Reason for fake dating established
2. Pretense begins
3. Forced intimacy in public performance
4. Real feelings emerging beneath the act
5. Pretense exposed or moment of truth
6. Genuine relationship begins
Description: Two characters pretend to be in a relationship for some external reason and develop real feelings during the performance. Highly conventionalized rom-com staple.
Exemplars: To All the Boys I've Loved Before; The Proposal; The Wedding Date; 10 Things I Hate About You.

## 403. Slow Burn Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Initial spark or notice
2. Long period of restraint or denial
3. Each chapter or episode adds incremental closeness
4. Major obstacle delays union
5. Eventual confession
6. Consummation that has been earned by accumulated weight
Description: Romance whose pleasure is the extended development of feeling — sometimes across multiple novels or seasons — with delayed consummation. Commitment to deferral.
Exemplars: Pride and Prejudice (canonical); Persuasion; many TV-series romances (The Office, Bones).

## 404. Workplace Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Colleagues introduced in workplace
2. Initial professional or competitive dynamic
3. Forced proximity through projects
4. Attraction breaks professional veneer
5. Workplace consequences (promotion, transfer, scandal)
6. Resolution: pursuit of relationship even at career cost
Description: Romance set in a shared workplace — colleague dynamics, professional ambition, the workplace's rules complicating the relationship.
Exemplars: Sally Thorne's The Hating Game; The Office (Jim and Pam); many romance novels and films.

## 405. Star-Crossed Lovers
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cosmic-scale obstacle (warring families, opposing kingdoms, fate)
2. Lovers meet
3. Love forms knowing the obstacle
4. Defiance of fate
5. Consequences fall
6. Tragic separation or shared death
Description: Romance whose external impossibility is woven into the universe itself — fate, family feud, war, religious schism. Cosmic-fatalistic register.
Exemplars: Romeo and Juliet; West Side Story; Tristan and Iseult; The English Patient.

## 406. Fated Mates
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Supernatural framework with mate-bond established
2. Encounter triggers recognition
3. Mutual pull resisted or accepted
4. Bond confirmation (often physical/magical)
5. External threat to the bond
6. Permanent mating bond established
Description: Romance (typically paranormal) in which two characters are cosmically destined for each other — recognition is biological, magical, or supernatural. Inevitability premise.
Exemplars: Many shifter romance novels; J.R. Ward's Black Dagger Brotherhood (variants); Maya Banks; Nalini Singh's Psy-Changeling.

## 407. Forbidden Love (deeper sub-form)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Power-asymmetric relationship established
2. Mutual recognition develops
3. Fight against feelings
4. Crossing the line
5. Discovery threatened
6. Choice: relationship at career/social cost, or end
Description: Love forbidden by specific institutional or social structure — student-teacher, priest-parishioner, employer-employee — with the prohibition itself becoming the structural drama.
Exemplars: The Reader (Schlink); Notes on a Scandal; Y tu mamá también; Lust, Caution (Ang Lee).

## 408. Love Triangle
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's existing relationship (or first interest)
2. Second interest enters
3. Genuine attraction to both
4. Comparison and conflict
5. Crisis forces choice
6. Choice made; one interest left behind
Description: Romance with three principals where the protagonist must choose between two romantic interests. Specific three-corner structure — choice itself is the genre's content.
Exemplars: The Hunger Games (Peeta/Gale); Twilight (Jacob/Edward); Bridget Jones's Diary; The Age of Innocence.

## 409. Hate-to-Love
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Loud antagonism on first meeting
2. Forced collaboration
3. First moment of grudging respect
4. Sexual/romantic tension breaks through
5. Confession of feelings
6. Hot reconciliation
Description: Specific enemies-to-lovers variant where antagonism is initial and dramatic but transformation faster and more passionate. Accelerated arc, intense register.
Exemplars: Christina Lauren's novels; Tessa Bailey; many shorter romance novels and films.

## 410. Sweet / Clean Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lovers meet
2. Wholesome attraction
3. Conflicts external (family, work, faith)
4. Tested but stays clean
5. Profession of love
6. Wedding-shaped close
Description: Romance without explicit sexuality and graphic content — typically targeted at religious, conservative, or younger audiences. Register of restraint.
Exemplars: Bethany House Christian romance; Hallmark Channel films; Karen Kingsbury's novels; Amish romance subgenre.

## 411. Bodice Ripper / Old-School Historical Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Heroine in vulnerable position
2. Powerful, often dangerous hero
3. Initial coercion or capture
4. Reluctant attraction
5. Hero's love revealed beneath the dominance
6. Mutual passion and union
Description: 1970s-80s historical romance with sexual content treated as central — often featuring rakes, pirates, captive heroines. Now somewhat dated; the genre has evolved.
Exemplars: Kathleen Woodiwiss's The Flame and the Flower (1972 foundational); early Johanna Lindsey; early Bertrice Small.

## 412. Erotic Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Sexually-charged meeting
2. Sexual tension built
3. Consummation early
4. Emotional connection deepens through physical
5. External crisis tests both
6. Committed relationship and ongoing intimacy
Description: Romance with explicit sexual content as central to the relationship's development. Distinct from generic romance by foregrounding sexuality; from erotica by romance arc remaining primary.
Exemplars: E.L. James's Fifty Shades of Grey (controversial); Christina Lauren; Lauren Blakely; Lisa Renee Jones.

## 413. Queer Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Queer protagonist's life/identity context
2. Romantic interest meets
3. Attraction develops with possible identity stakes
4. External or internal conflict (family, religion, self-acceptance)
5. Confession and risk
6. Committed relationship
Description: Romance featuring same-sex or LGBTQ+ partners. Specific cultural and identity context; sub-conventions vary by sexuality.
Exemplars: Casey McQuiston's Red, White & Royal Blue; Becky Albertalli's Simon vs. the Homo Sapiens Agenda; T.J. Klune's The House in the Cerulean Sea; Alice Oseman's Heartstopper.

## 414. Office Romance / Bossy Boss
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Employee's position established
2. Boss enters with authority
3. Forced collaboration on high-stakes project
4. Attraction crossing power lines
5. Affair begins; institutional risk
6. Choice — leave job, leave love, or change power dynamic
Description: Specific workplace-romance variant with power asymmetry between boss and employee. Forbidden-power register within professional setting.
Exemplars: Fifty Shades; many Harlequin Presents novels; Suits (Mike and Rachel).

## 415. Romance with Disability / Chronic Illness
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's life and condition established without pity
2. Romantic interest enters
3. Negotiating intimacy across the condition
4. Crisis (medical or relational)
5. Mutual vulnerability shown
6. Committed relationship that integrates the condition
Description: Romance in which one or both partners has a disability, chronic illness, or neurodivergence. Specific stakes around accommodation, vulnerability, dignity.
Exemplars: Helen Hoang's The Kiss Quotient (autism); Talia Hibbert; Jojo Moyes's Me Before You (controversial); The Fault in Our Stars.

## 416. Mafia / Dark Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Heroine enters dangerous criminal world
2. Mafia/dangerous hero takes interest
3. Possessive courtship
4. Heroine's danger as liability or asset
5. Climactic violence
6. Loyalty and protection sealed
Description: Romance with criminal antihero — mafia, mobster, dangerous outlaw — as romantic lead. Morally compromised hero and dark register.
Exemplars: Penelope Sky; Sylvia Day's Bared to You; many "dark romance" indie novels; The Godfather's romantic subplots (proto).

## 417. Polyamorous / Reverse Harem Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist and first partner
2. Second partner enters; jealousy or compatibility
3. Decision to expand the relationship
4. Negotiating multi-partner dynamics
5. External crisis tests the arrangement
6. Stable polyamorous bond
Description: Romance with multiple romantic partners in non-monogamous arrangements — reverse harem (one woman, multiple men) or genuine polyamory. Multi-partner structure.
Exemplars: Various indie romance novels; Quinn Loftis novels (variants); polyamorous-focused romance subgenre.

## 418. Spy Thriller / Espionage
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Operative introduced with their distinctive method
2. Mission assigned
3. Investigation and tradecraft
4. Double-cross or unexpected complication
5. Confrontation with antagonist intelligence service
6. Mission resolved — at moral or personal cost
Description: Espionage narrative featuring intelligence operatives, double-crosses, and geopolitical stakes. Sub-types: glamorous (Bond), morally ambiguous (le Carré), procedural (Deighton).
Exemplars: Ian Fleming's Bond novels; John le Carré's The Spy Who Came in from the Cold; Len Deighton's The IPCRESS File; Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy.

## 419. Legal Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lawyer protagonist takes on the case
2. Investigation reveals deeper layers
3. Powerful antagonist (firm, corporation, state) emerges
4. Personal threat to the protagonist
5. Courtroom climax
6. Justice — won, denied, or compromised
Description: Thriller centered on legal stakes — courtroom, firm, case. Adversarial structure; justice contested through legal mechanics.
Exemplars: John Grisham's The Firm, A Time to Kill; Scott Turow's Presumed Innocent; Robert Traver's Anatomy of a Murder.

## 420. Medical Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Medical setting with patient or specimen
2. Anomaly detected — disease, contamination, conspiracy
3. Doctor-protagonist investigates
4. Resistance from institution
5. Climactic medical crisis
6. Resolution with implications for patients/public
Description: Thriller in medical settings where stakes are biological — outbreaks, dangerous treatments, hospital conspiracies. Doctor as detective.
Exemplars: Robin Cook's Coma; Michael Crichton's The Andromeda Strain; Tess Gerritsen's medical thrillers; The Constant Gardener (le Carré).

## 421. Heist narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Target introduced and its impossibility established
2. Mastermind assembles team
3. Each specialist's skill highlighted
4. Plan elaborated
5. Execution begins
6. Unexpected complication forces improvisation
7. Theft completed (or fails) — often with twist
Description: A team of specialists plans and executes an elaborate theft. Highly structured genre with established conventions. Plan-meets-reality is the structural pleasure.
Exemplars: Ocean's Eleven; The Sting; Topkapi; Heat; Inception (heist-shaped); Money Heist (La Casa de Papel).

## 422. Disaster narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Multiple character introductions before the disaster
2. Warning signs — sometimes ignored
3. Disaster strikes
4. Survival actions taken
5. Deaths and survivals
6. Aftermath — what is rebuilt, what is lost
Description: Narrative organized around large-scale catastrophe — natural disaster, plague, accident — and a cast of characters facing it. The disaster itself, multi-perspective.
Exemplars: The Towering Inferno; Titanic (Cameron); Contagion; Sebastian Junger's The Perfect Storm.

## 423. Action Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hero with action skills established
2. Threat that requires action
3. First action sequence
4. Escalating set-pieces
5. Climactic large action
6. Resolution with minimum exposition
Description: Thriller foregrounding physical action — chases, shootouts, fights — with plot at the service of set-pieces. Kinesthetic sequences.
Exemplars: Lee Child's Jack Reacher novels; Vince Flynn's Mitch Rapp; Mission: Impossible franchise; Bourne franchise.

## 424. Eco-Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Environmental anomaly introduced
2. Scientist or activist notices
3. Establishment dismisses
4. Crisis accelerates
5. Climactic event with environmental cause
6. Aftermath and warning
Description: Thriller in which environmental crisis drives the action — climate disasters, pandemics, ecological warfare. Environmental causality.
Exemplars: Michael Crichton's State of Fear; Daniel Quinn's Ishmael; The Day After Tomorrow; Geostorm; Robinson's eco-fiction.

## 425. Hostage Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hostages taken
2. Negotiator enters
3. Demands and counter-demands
4. Building tension
5. Critical moment (raid, accident, negotiation)
6. Resolution — release, raid, or tragedy
Description: Thriller in which a hostage situation drives the entire narrative — limited setting, ticking clock, negotiation as primary action. Spatial-temporal constraint.
Exemplars: Dog Day Afternoon; The Negotiator; Inside Man; Phone Booth.

## 426. Disaster Thriller (prevention)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Warning signs noticed by expert
2. Establishment dismisses
3. Disaster begins
4. Race to mitigate or evacuate
5. Climactic disaster moment
6. Lessons learned (or not)
Description: Thriller in which a large-scale disaster (natural, industrial, terrorist) is imminent or unfolding. Distinct from disaster narrative by thriller-pace and prevention-focus.
Exemplars: Deep Impact; Armageddon; Sully; Apollo 13; Crichton's Sphere (variant).

## 427. Financial Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Financial instrument or institution introduced
2. Anomaly detected
3. Investigation reveals fraud or threat
4. Powerful financial antagonist
5. Climactic maneuver (trade, exposure, market move)
6. Reckoning with monetary consequence
Description: Thriller centered on high-stakes financial maneuvering — markets, fraud, banking conspiracies, hostile takeovers. Specifically economic content.
Exemplars: The Big Short (Lewis); Tom Wolfe's Bonfire of the Vanities; Margin Call; Wall Street (1987); John Grisham's The Firm.

## 428. Religious Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Religious artifact or text discovered
2. Mystery surrounds it
3. Religious institution involved
4. Hidden faction emerges
5. Race to control or expose
6. Implications for faith or world
Description: Thriller in which religious texts, artifacts, conspiracies, or eschatology drive the plot. Often involves Vatican, Templars, ancient sects, or apocalyptic prophecy.
Exemplars: Dan Brown's The Da Vinci Code and Angels & Demons; Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose (literary variant); The Omen (horror variant).

## 429. Forensic Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Body or evidence with mystery
2. Forensic specialist begins analysis
3. Each test narrows possibilities
4. Pattern reveals serial or organized criminality
5. Forensic insight identifies perpetrator
6. Thriller-paced confrontation
Description: Thriller foregrounding forensic science — crime scene analysis, autopsy, DNA, ballistics — as investigation's primary tool. Higher-stakes, more thriller-paced register than forensic procedural.
Exemplars: Patricia Cornwell's Kay Scarpetta; Kathy Reichs's Tempe Brennan; Karin Slaughter; Bones (TV).

## 430. Cyber Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cyber breach detected
2. Investigator (often hacker) called
3. Exploration of digital traces
4. Real-world stakes revealed (infrastructure, finance)
5. Climactic digital-physical confrontation
6. Vulnerability patched (or exploited)
Description: Thriller in which the central crime, conflict, or threat is cyber — hacking, data theft, infrastructure attack. Digital register.
Exemplars: Daniel Suarez's Daemon; Mr. Robot; Stieg Larsson's Dragon Tattoo trilogy (variant); Sneakers (1992).

## 431. Whistleblower Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Insider discovers wrongdoing
2. Decides to leak/expose
3. First exposure attempt
4. Institutional retaliation begins
5. Pursuit — physical or legal
6. Truth published; cost paid
Description: Thriller in which the protagonist is an insider exposing institutional wrongdoing — and is hunted for it. Action-thriller pace and pursuit register.
Exemplars: The Insider (Wigand-Mann); Snowden (Stone); All the President's Men (variant); The Pelican Brief.

## 432. Survival Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist stranded or hunted
2. Initial survival inventory
3. First major challenge (weather, predator, terrain)
4. Resource depletion and improvisation
5. Climactic test
6. Survival, rescue, or ambiguous close
Description: Thriller in which protagonist must survive in extreme environment or against ruthless pursuit — wilderness, ocean, post-apocalyptic terrain. Less supernatural than survival horror.
Exemplars: 127 Hours (Aron Ralston); Jon Krakauer's Into Thin Air; Cast Away; The Revenant; Open Water.

## 433. Erotic Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Encounter with sexually-charged figure
2. Affair begins
3. Strange occurrences emerge
4. Lover's true nature suspected
5. Climactic confrontation tied to sexuality
6. Survival, with damage
Description: Thriller in which sexuality is woven into the danger — the protagonist's desire IS partly the threat. Distinct from erotic romance by menacing register.
Exemplars: Basic Instinct; Body Heat; Fatal Attraction; Gone Girl (variant).

## 434. Domestic Noir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent domestic stability
2. Narrator's unreliable framing emerges
3. Past or hidden truth surfaces
4. Spouse/family revealed differently
5. Twist that recontextualizes
6. New equilibrium with damage
Description: Crossover of domestic thriller and noir — middle-class home life with hidden darkness, unreliable narrators, twist endings. Noir-influenced literary style.
Exemplars: Gillian Flynn's Gone Girl; A.J. Finn's The Woman in the Window; Paula Hawkins; Liane Moriarty.

## 435. Political Conspiracy Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Anomaly noticed by political insider
2. Investigation triggers warnings
3. Deeper conspiracy revealed
4. Friends/witnesses dying
5. Race to expose before being silenced
6. Truth exposed at heavy cost
Description: Sub-form combining political and conspiracy thrillers — a hidden political plot involving high-level officials. Specifically political-power conspiracy.
Exemplars: Robert Ludlum's Bourne novels; The Manchurian Candidate; No Way Out; David Baldacci's political thrillers.

## 436. Adventure (sub-form)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hero called to dangerous expedition
2. Team gathered
3. Departure into the wilderness
4. Dangers and discoveries
5. Climactic confrontation with environment or rival
6. Return (often with reduced team) carrying knowledge or treasure
Description: Variant focused on physical exploration of dangerous geography — jungles, polar wastes, deep sea, mountains. Travelogue-with-stakes register.
Exemplars: Jack London's stories; Jon Krakauer; H. Rider Haggard; Indiana Jones.

## 437. Survival Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-trauma life
2. Onset of trauma/danger
3. Survival actions taken
4. Lowest point and breakthrough
5. Eventual escape or rescue
6. Reflection on what survival means
Description: First-person account of an extreme survival experience told in memoir form — cancer, mountaineering accident, war, abuse. Non-fiction register.
Exemplars: Jon Krakauer's Into Thin Air; Aron Ralston's Between a Rock and a Hard Place; Joe Simpson's Touching the Void; Ernest Shackleton's South.

## 438. Techno-thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Advanced technology introduced (military, scientific, computing)
2. Threat tied to technology emerges
3. Expert protagonist deploys technical knowledge
4. Cat-and-mouse with adversary using same tech
5. Climactic technical confrontation
6. Resolution with implications for tech development
Description: Thriller foregrounding high technology — military hardware, computing, biotech — as both setting and engine. Technical detail as feature.
Exemplars: Tom Clancy's Hunt for Red October; Michael Crichton's novels; Daniel Suarez's Daemon; The Andromeda Strain.

## 439. Crime Thriller (general)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crime introduced with high stakes
2. Protagonist (criminal, victim, or detective) drawn in
3. Pursuit or escalation begins
4. Twists complicate
5. Climactic confrontation
6. Resolution with criminal-justice consequences
Description: Broad thriller form centered on criminal activity — crime planning, pursuit, or aftermath. Distinguished from procedural by thriller-pace.
Exemplars: Don Winslow's The Power of the Dog; James Ellroy; Dennis Lehane's Mystic River; Heat.

## 440. Slow-Burn Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Quiet setting and protagonist established
2. Subtle wrongness noticed
3. Tension builds across long sequences
4. Increasingly menacing detail
5. Climactic violent or revelatory moment
6. Aftermath that unsettles more than resolves
Description: Thriller that develops menace gradually rather than through action set-pieces. Atmosphere-heavy, dread-driven register.
Exemplars: No Country for Old Men (McCarthy/Coens); Prisoners; Wind River; Patricia Highsmith's literary thrillers.

## 441. Ancient World Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ancient setting evoked through detail
2. Historical figure or invented protagonist embedded in real events
3. Period-specific obstacle (war, succession, plague)
4. Personal arc within historical events
5. Climactic moment tied to real history
6. Implications for what we know happened
Description: Historical fiction set in ancient civilizations — Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, Persia, China, India. Specific archaeological-historical detail.
Exemplars: Mary Renault's The King Must Die; Robert Graves's I, Claudius; Steven Pressfield's Gates of Fire; Madeline Miller's Circe; Colleen McCullough's Masters of Rome.

## 442. Medieval Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Medieval social-political setting evoked
2. Protagonist within feudal or religious structure
3. Crisis (war, plague, heresy)
4. Period-specific moral framework engaged
5. Climactic medieval event
6. Resolution within medieval terms
Description: Historical fiction set in the medieval period (~500-1500 CE) — knights, monasteries, plagues, courts, peasant revolts. Medieval-specific detail and worldview.
Exemplars: Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose; Ken Follett's The Pillars of the Earth; Bernard Cornwell's The Last Kingdom; The Saxon Stories.

## 443. Renaissance / Tudor Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Court or city-state setting
2. Royal/religious power dynamics
3. Protagonist navigates patronage and intrigue
4. Religious or political crisis
5. Climactic court event (execution, marriage, war)
6. Period's transformation reflected
Description: Historical fiction set in European Renaissance and Tudor periods (~1400-1600). Court-intrigue heavy; era's politics, religion, art are central.
Exemplars: Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall trilogy (Cromwell); Philippa Gregory's The Other Boleyn Girl; C.J. Sansom's Shardlake mysteries.

## 444. Victorian / Edwardian Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Victorian/Edwardian social setting
2. Class or imperial conflict
3. Period scientific/social change as backdrop
4. Personal scandal or transgression
5. Climactic event tied to era
6. Hint of coming 20th-century rupture
Description: Historical fiction set in Victorian and Edwardian eras (~1837-1914). Industrial-imperial setting, class anxieties, specific period social codes.
Exemplars: A.S. Byatt's Possession; Charles Palliser's The Quincunx; Sarah Waters's Fingersmith; The French Lieutenant's Woman (Fowles).

## 445. WWI Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-war innocence
2. Volunteering or conscription
3. Trench warfare horror
4. Loss of comrades, faith, illusion
5. Climactic battle or wound
6. Survivor's broken peace
Description: Fiction set during or around World War I (1914-18). Trench warfare register, lost-generation themes, modernist undertones.
Exemplars: Erich Maria Remarque's All Quiet on the Western Front; Pat Barker's Regeneration trilogy; Sebastian Faulks's Birdsong; Testament of Youth (Brittain).

## 446. WWII Historical Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-war or early-war world
2. War's intrusion on protagonist
3. Specific war experience (combat, occupation, hiding, work)
4. Moral test under wartime pressure
5. Climactic war event
6. Survival and reckoning
Description: Fiction set during or around World War II (1939-45). Largest historical-fiction subgenre by volume. Spans military, resistance, Holocaust, home-front, post-war.
Exemplars: Anthony Doerr's All the Light We Cannot See; Markus Zusak's The Book Thief; Heller's Catch-22 (variant); Schindler's List (Keneally).

## 447. Holocaust Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-Holocaust life established
2. Persecution begins
3. Loss of rights, home, family
4. Camp, hiding, or transit
5. Survival or witness
6. Reckoning — for those who lived, and for those who didn't
Description: Fiction or memoir set during the Holocaust (Shoah). Specific moral and historical weight; ethical conventions about representation are part of the genre.
Exemplars: Elie Wiesel's Night; Primo Levi's If This Is a Man; Anne Frank's Diary; Imre Kertész's Fatelessness; Schindler's List.

## 448. Cold War Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cold War era situation
2. Ideological lines drawn
3. Espionage or political maneuvering
4. Personal stakes within geopolitical pressure
5. Climactic moment of confrontation
6. New geopolitical configuration (or accommodation)
Description: Fiction set during Cold War (1947-91), focused on ideological-geopolitical conflict between West and Soviet bloc. Spy thriller, political fiction, lived experience.
Exemplars: John le Carré's Karla trilogy; Don DeLillo's Libra; Robert Harris's Archangel; The Americans (TV); Bridge of Spies.

## 449. American Civil War Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-war society or family
2. War divides
3. Battle or home-front experience
4. Loss, conflict over values, racial reckoning
5. Climactic battle or moment
6. Reconstruction and lasting wounds
Description: Fiction set during or around the U.S. Civil War (1861-65). Specific North-South political and racial context.
Exemplars: Stephen Crane's The Red Badge of Courage; Margaret Mitchell's Gone with the Wind; Charles Frazier's Cold Mountain; Beloved (Morrison, post-war variant).

## 450. Western (frontier narrative)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Frontier town or landscape
2. Outsider or conflict arrives
3. Tension between law and disorder
4. Showdown setup
5. Gunfight or confrontation
6. Resolution: justice, exile, or ambiguous closure
Description: Fiction set in the American West during frontier era (~1860s-1900s) — gunfighters, sheriffs, settlers, displaced Native peoples, ranchers vs farmers.
Exemplars: Owen Wister's The Virginian; McCarthy's Blood Meridian and No Country for Old Men; Larry McMurtry's Lonesome Dove; True Grit (Portis).

## 451. Revisionist Western
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Conventional Western setup
2. Subversion or complication
3. Antiheroes or compromised heroes
4. Violence with consequences
5. Climactic showdown that lacks moral clarity
6. End of frontier mythology
Description: Western that subverts traditional frontier mythology — Native dispossession, racial complexity, ambiguous heroism, costs of Manifest Destiny. Critical posture.
Exemplars: McCarthy's Blood Meridian; Unforgiven (Eastwood); Dances with Wolves; The Sisters Brothers; Sergio Leone's Once Upon a Time in the West.

## 452. Imperial / Colonial Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Colonial outpost or imperial heart
2. Encounter with colonized people/place
3. Power asymmetry and its costs
4. Crisis (rebellion, scandal, disease)
5. Climactic confrontation with imperial logic
6. Empire's continuation, decline, or recognition of guilt
Description: Fiction set during European imperial-colonial expansion or its decline — colonizers' perspectives, colonial subjects' resistance, the colonial encounter's costs.
Exemplars: E.M. Forster's A Passage to India; Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness; Adichie's Half of a Yellow Sun; Amitav Ghosh's Sea of Poppies.

## 453. Post-Colonial Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Colonial or post-colonial society introduced
2. Protagonist's identity within colonial framework
3. Encounter with colonial legacy
4. Trauma or recovery of culture
5. Reckoning with the colonial wound
6. Articulation of post-colonial identity
Description: Fiction by formerly-colonized peoples about colonialism's experience and aftermath. Writing from the subject of empire rather than its agents.
Exemplars: Achebe's Things Fall Apart; Rushdie's Midnight's Children; Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's A Grain of Wheat; Dangarembga's Nervous Conditions; Kincaid's A Small Place.

## 454. Regency / Napoleonic Historical
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Regency-era setting
2. Napoleonic war as background or foreground
3. Society at home
4. Battle or political event
5. Personal arc tied to era
6. Aftermath of Waterloo
Description: Specific period historical fiction set during British Regency / Napoleonic Wars (~1795-1820). Distinct from Regency romance by foregrounding the broader historical drama.
Exemplars: Bernard Cornwell's Sharpe series; Patrick O'Brian's Aubrey-Maturin novels; Naomi Novik's Temeraire (fantasy variant).

## 455. Roaring Twenties / Jazz Age
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. 1920s milieu — wealth, parties, music
2. Protagonist's place within or outside it
3. Aspirational pursuit
4. Hidden corruption or hollowness
5. Climactic disillusion
6. Crash hinted or arrived
Description: Fiction set in the 1920s — boom, jazz, prohibition, modernism. Atmospheres of excess, hidden hollow, impending crash.
Exemplars: F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby; Anita Loos's Gentlemen Prefer Blondes; Boardwalk Empire (TV); Babylon (Chazelle).

## 456. Great Depression Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-Depression or early-Depression life
2. Economic collapse hits
3. Loss of home, work, community
4. Survival on the road or in the slum
5. Encounter with collective struggle
6. Endurance and dignity preserved
Description: Fiction set during the Great Depression (~1929-39). Poverty, displacement, labor. Often paired with social-realist register.
Exemplars: Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath; James Agee's Let Us Now Praise Famous Men; Erskine Caldwell's Tobacco Road.

## 457. Civil Rights Era Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Segregated society introduced
2. Movement begins or arrives
3. Protagonist's choice to engage or resist
4. Confrontation with structural racism
5. Climactic event (march, sit-in, bombing)
6. Cost paid; movement continues
Description: Fiction set during the American Civil Rights Movement (~1954-68). Specific political-racial register and historical movement frame.
Exemplars: Harper Lee's To Kill a Mockingbird; Toni Morrison's novels (variant); Selma (DuVernay); The Help (Stockett, controversial); Whitehead's The Nickel Boys.

## 458. Vietnam War Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-war young protagonist
2. Drafted or volunteers
3. Vietnam combat experience
4. Moral collapse or testing
5. Climactic war moment
6. Return: changed, broken, or guilty
Description: Fiction set during or around Vietnam War (~1955-75). Jungle warfare, moral ambiguity, generational rupture, post-traumatic register.
Exemplars: Tim O'Brien's The Things They Carried; Karl Marlantes's Matterhorn; Michael Herr's Dispatches; Apocalypse Now; Platoon.

## 459. Soviet / Eastern Bloc Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Soviet society's surface
2. Crack in the official narrative
3. Risk of dissent or hidden truth
4. Encounter with state apparatus
5. Climactic confrontation (camp, exile, defection)
6. Survival or destruction within the system
Description: Fiction set in Soviet Union or Eastern Bloc — gulag, surveillance, dissident underground, communal apartments. Specific totalitarian-state register.
Exemplars: Solzhenitsyn's One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich; Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita; Kundera's The Unbearable Lightness of Being; Funder's Stasiland.

## 460. Reconstruction Era Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. End of war and beginning of new freedom
2. Hopes and first steps
3. White Southern reaction begins
4. Violence against Black communities
5. Federal withdrawal or compromise
6. Era ends in defeat and the long shadow forward
Description: Fiction set during American Reconstruction (~1865-77) — post-Civil-War South, ex-slaves' freedom and its limits, reactionary backlash. Specific historical-political moment.
Exemplars: Toni Morrison's Beloved; Edward P. Jones's The Known World; Whitehead's The Underground Railroad; Free State of Jones (film).

## 461. Stream of Consciousness Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Consciousness opens mid-thought
2. Associative chains rather than linear plot
3. Sensory, memory, and present interweave
4. Apparent fragments cohere into pattern
5. Climactic moment of clarity or breakdown
6. Consciousness closes mid-thought
Description: Modernist novel form rendering unfiltered flow of a character's thought — associative, non-linear, sensory, syntactically experimental. Interior-mimetic register.
Exemplars: James Joyce's Ulysses; Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway and To the Lighthouse; William Faulkner's The Sound and the Fury; Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time.

## 462. Modernist Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Disrupted chronology or perspective established
2. Multiple consciousnesses or voices
3. Symbolic and mythic underlay
4. Crisis of meaning or self
5. Modernist epiphany (small, ambiguous)
6. Closure that resists finality
Description: Early-20th-c. literary movement: experimental form, fragmented chronology, interior depth, skeptical of grand narratives. Formal innovation; coherent narrative ambition.
Exemplars: Joyce's Ulysses; Woolf's Mrs Dalloway; Hemingway's The Sun Also Rises; T.S. Eliot's The Waste Land.

## 463. Postmodernist Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Unstable narrative voice or frame
2. Metafictional intrusion
3. Pastiche or genre-mixing
4. Apparent coherence ironized
5. Climactic gesture that may or may not be sincere
6. Refusal of conclusive meaning
Description: Late-20th-c. literary movement: ironic, metafictional, anti-foundationalist, often humorous. Self-conscious play with the conventions of fiction itself.
Exemplars: Thomas Pynchon's Gravity's Rainbow; Italo Calvino's If on a Winter's Night a Traveler; Don DeLillo's White Noise; David Foster Wallace's Infinite Jest.

## 464. Maximalist Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Multiple narrative threads opened
2. Massive informational density
3. Cultural-historical scope unfurls
4. Threads loosely interweave
5. Climactic concentration of themes
6. Resolution not through plot but through accumulated weight
Description: Long, encyclopedic, dense literary novel that aims to encompass everything — culture, history, science, philosophy. Length, ambition, density.
Exemplars: Pynchon's Gravity's Rainbow; Wallace's Infinite Jest; Roberto Bolaño's 2666; DeLillo's Underworld; Joseph McElroy.

## 465. Minimalist Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Spare scene
2. Minimal action
3. Strong emotion implied through restraint
4. Telling small detail
5. Quiet shift
6. Closing image, often unresolved
Description: Late-20th-c. literary movement: stripped prose, restrained emotional register, omitted subtext, economy as principle. More literary than dirty realism.
Exemplars: Raymond Carver; Amy Hempel; Ann Beattie; Mary Robison; Frederick Barthelme.

## 466. Auto-Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Narrator clearly resembles author
2. Real biographical material used
3. Fictional elements introduced
4. Reader's uncertainty about fact/fiction
5. Insight whose truth status is multilayered
6. Closing that refuses to clarify
Description: Fiction in which author's biographical material is openly used while remaining fictional — line between novel and memoir is performatively blurred.
Exemplars: Karl Ove Knausgård's My Struggle; Rachel Cusk's Outline trilogy; Sheila Heti's How Should a Person Be?; Ben Lerner's 10:04; Annie Ernaux.

## 467. Roman à Clef
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparently fictional society introduced
2. Characters obviously based on real figures
3. Plot follows real events at a fiction's distance
4. Insight or judgment about real figures
5. Climactic event with real-world counterpart
6. Reader's pleasure: decoding
Description: A novel in which real people appear as fictional characters — typically with names changed. Distinct from auto-fiction by depicting OTHERS rather than the author primarily.
Exemplars: Truman Capote's Answered Prayers; Jacqueline Susann's Valley of the Dolls; Primary Colors (Joe Klein); Orwell's Animal Farm (allegorical variant).

## 468. Metafiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Narrative begins conventionally
2. First metafictional intrusion
3. Form's conventions made visible
4. Character or narrator addresses reader
5. Climactic moment that uses metafiction structurally
6. Resolution through fiction's own self-knowledge
Description: Fiction that explicitly draws attention to its own status as fiction — author intrudes, narrative comments on itself, characters break the fourth wall.
Exemplars: John Fowles's The French Lieutenant's Woman; Italo Calvino's If on a Winter's Night; Charlie Kaufman's Adaptation; The Princess Bride (Goldman).

## 469. Hysterical Realism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Many characters introduced rapidly
2. Improbable connections between them
3. Conspiracy or pattern hinted
4. Cultural-encyclopedic digressions
5. Climactic convergence
6. Sense of meaning that may evade
Description: Literary mode (named pejoratively by James Wood, 2000) characterized by manic energy, large casts, sprawling plots, conspiracy themes, information density.
Exemplars: Zadie Smith's White Teeth; Salman Rushdie's The Ground Beneath Her Feet; Jonathan Franzen's The Corrections (variant); DeLillo's Underworld.

## 470. New Sincerity
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparently ironic situation
2. Refusal of full ironic distance
3. Sincere emotional engagement
4. Vulnerable disclosure
5. Earnest moment that risks looking foolish
6. Affirmation of feeling without condescension
Description: Late-20th/early-21st-c. literary movement reacting against postmodern irony — embracing sincerity, earnestness, emotional directness. Post-ironic register.
Exemplars: David Foster Wallace's later essays; Dave Eggers's A Heartbreaking Work of Staggering Genius; George Saunders's later fiction; Sally Rooney (in part).

## 471. Philosophical Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Philosophical question or framework introduced
2. Characters embody competing positions
3. Action serves the philosophical inquiry
4. Crisis tests the philosophy
5. Insight or aporia reached
6. Resolution through intellectual rather than plot mechanics
Description: Novel whose primary content is philosophical inquiry — characters wrestle explicitly with metaphysical, ethical, or political questions. Foregrounds ideas as content.
Exemplars: Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov; Sartre's Nausea; Camus's The Plague; Kundera's The Unbearable Lightness of Being; Iris Murdoch.

## 472. Existentialist Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist in disorienting world
2. Encounter with absurdity
3. Recognition of cosmic meaninglessness
4. Burden of choice without external grounding
5. Authentic act (or refusal)
6. Living-with the recognition
Description: Mid-20th-c. literary movement: protagonists confront meaninglessness, absurdity, burden of freedom. Specific existential framework.
Exemplars: Camus's The Stranger; Sartre's Nausea; Dostoevsky's Notes from Underground; Kafka's The Trial (proto); Beckett.

## 473. Absurdist Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent normality
2. Absurd event treated as ordinary
3. Logic's failure to encompass
4. Characters carry on as if logic still held
5. Climactic full collapse of logical structure
6. Continuation, perhaps with grim laugh
Description: Fiction grounded in recognition that human existence is absurd — meaningless or irreducible to logic. Leans into comedy and play rather than gravity.
Exemplars: Kafka's stories and The Trial; Beckett's Waiting for Godot; Eugene Ionesco; Daniil Kharms; Catch-22.

## 474. Surrealist Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Dream-logic established
2. Image or event without rational connection
3. Sequence built by association
4. Symbolic charge accumulates
5. Climactic surreal moment
6. Awakening or non-awakening
Description: Fiction grounded in dream-logic, irrational juxtaposition, the unconscious. Specific Surrealist-movement aesthetics — Bretonian automatism, dreamlike imagery.
Exemplars: André Breton's Nadja; Leonora Carrington; Bruno Schulz's The Street of Crocodiles; Haruki Murakami (proto-surrealist variant).

## 475. Magical Realism (literary, beyond LA)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Realist setting established
2. Magical element introduced without comment
3. Characters accept it as ordinary
4. Magic and politics/history intertwine
5. Cumulative weight transforms surface
6. Closing in the ongoing magical-real
Description: Magical realism as a global literary mode — fantastic elements treated as ordinary within otherwise realistic settings. Distinct from Latin American specific genre as a transferred mode.
Exemplars: Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children; Toni Morrison's Beloved; Haruki Murakami's Kafka on the Shore; Yann Martel's Life of Pi.

## 476. Gothic Literary Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Atmospheric setting (often house or estate)
2. Protagonist arrives or returns
3. Hidden family or local secret hinted
4. Investigation into the past
5. Climactic revelation, sometimes spectral
6. Resolution that may not fully exorcise
Description: Literary fiction drawing on gothic conventions — atmospheric darkness, family secrets, decaying houses, spectral presences — without committing fully to horror genre.
Exemplars: Donna Tartt's The Little Friend; Wuthering Heights; Rebecca; Sarah Waters's The Little Stranger; Eleanor Catton's The Luminaries.

## 477. Campus Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Campus setting and academic hierarchy
2. Protagonist (student or faculty) navigates institution
3. Romantic, intellectual, or professional intrigue
4. Academic crisis (scandal, tenure, exam)
5. Climactic confrontation with institutional logic
6. Term ends, hierarchy continues
Description: Novel set in a university — students, professors, the politics of academia. Specific institutional setting and its peculiar mores. Often comic or satirical.
Exemplars: Kingsley Amis's Lucky Jim; David Lodge's campus trilogy; Donna Tartt's The Secret History; Wonder Boys (Chabon); The Idiot (Batuman).

## 478. Künstlerroman (artist's novel)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Young protagonist's first creative impulse
2. Encounter with art that calls them
3. Apprenticeship or struggle to develop voice
4. Crisis of vocation or recognition
5. Mature artistic accomplishment
6. Affirmation of vocation as identity
Description: Specific Bildungsroman variant: novel of an artist's development — formation of the creative self. Foregrounds artistic vocation as the central arc.
Exemplars: James Joyce's A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man; Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time; Maugham's Of Human Bondage; Woolf's To the Lighthouse (in part).

## 479. Anti-Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent novel-opening
2. Conventions undermined
3. No clear protagonist or progression
4. Refusal of identification or sympathy
5. Climactic moment that resists climax
6. Closure refused
Description: Novel that systematically refuses or subverts the conventions of the novel — plot, character development, resolution. Specific anti-posture.
Exemplars: Nathalie Sarraute's Tropisms; Alain Robbe-Grillet's Jealousy; Beckett's The Unnamable; David Markson's Wittgenstein's Mistress.

## 480. Verse Novel
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Verse opening establishes narrator
2. Story unfolds across stanzas/chapters
3. Lyric intensification at key moments
4. Climactic poetic moment
5. Resolution through compressed verse
6. Closing image with poetic finality
Description: A novel-length narrative composed entirely or primarily in verse. Formal hybrid: novel scope, poetry's compression and music.
Exemplars: Pushkin's Eugene Onegin (foundational); Vikram Seth's The Golden Gate; Anne Carson's Autobiography of Red; Jacqueline Woodson's Brown Girl Dreaming.

## 481. Mystery Play (medieval)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Biblical scene set up in vernacular
2. Comic interludes mixed with sacred
3. Dramatic biblical episode
4. Moral application made plain
5. Closing with transition to next cycle
6. Audience procession to next station
Description: Medieval cycle drama dramatizing biblical episodes — Creation, Noah, Nativity, Passion, Judgment. Performed by guilds in towns. Religious-pageant register.
Exemplars: York Mystery Plays; N-Town Plays; Towneley Plays; Coventry Mystery Plays.

## 482. Morality Play
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Everyman summoned by Death
2. Search for companion to the grave
3. Goods, Friends, Family abandon
4. Knowledge and Good Deeds remain
5. Confession and absolution
6. Death as sleep into salvation
Description: Late medieval allegorical drama in which Everyman or similar protagonist meets allegorical figures (Death, Goods, Knowledge, Mercy) and must make moral choices for salvation.
Exemplars: Everyman (c. 1500, canonical); The Castle of Perseverance; Mankind.

## 483. Closet Drama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setup that may resist staging
2. Long verse speeches
3. Internal action over external
4. Philosophical or political confrontation
5. Climactic verse
6. Resolution through meditation
Description: Drama written for reading rather than performance — often verse, philosophically dense, formally restrained. Non-performance ambition.
Exemplars: Seneca's tragedies; Goethe's Faust (in part); Byron's Manfred; Shelley's Prometheus Unbound.

## 484. Verse Drama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Verse-opening establishes register
2. Action through speech rather than scene-mechanics
3. Verbal confrontation as the central event
4. Soliloquies for interiority
5. Climactic verse exchange
6. Closing in verse
Description: Drama composed in verse rather than prose — highly artificial register that elevates speech. The Renaissance default; rare in modern drama.
Exemplars: Shakespeare's plays; T.S. Eliot's Murder in the Cathedral; Christopher Fry; Tony Kushner's Angels in America (in part).

## 485. Melodrama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent heroine introduced
2. Villain who threatens her
3. Hero who defends
4. Series of sensational threats
5. Climactic rescue or peril
6. Virtue rewarded; villain punished
Description: 19th-c. theatrical genre featuring sensationalism, clear good-and-evil, music-underscored emotion, broad acting. Emotional excess as feature, not flaw.
Exemplars: East Lynne; Uncle Tom's Cabin (theatrical adaptations); Way Down East; many silent-era films.

## 486. Well-Made Play
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Exposition establishes secret
2. Inciting event activates secret's pressure
3. Rising action through complications
4. Reversal that turns the situation
5. Recognition / scène-à-faire (obligatory scene)
6. Resolution through revelation
Description: 19th-c. dramatic structure formalized by Eugène Scribe: tightly plotted, with planted secrets, reversals, and complex denouement-through-revelation. Mechanical precision.
Exemplars: Scribe's plays; Sardou's plays; Ibsen's early structure; influenced later well-made cinema.

## 487. Naturalist Drama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Working-class or middle-class setting
2. Environmental and hereditary pressures shown
3. Character struggle within determinism
4. Inevitable trajectory
5. Crisis of social condition
6. Bleak resolution within naturalist frame
Description: Late-19th-c. drama applying naturalist principles to theater — characters determined by environment and heredity, ordinary people as protagonists, explicit social problems.
Exemplars: Ibsen's Ghosts; Strindberg's Miss Julie; Hauptmann's The Weavers; Chekhov (variant).

## 488. Symbolist Drama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Atmospheric, dreamlike setting
2. Ambiguous symbolic figures
3. Action carries hidden meaning
4. Symbolic crisis or revelation
5. Mood-driven climax
6. Closing in poetic ambiguity
Description: Late-19th-c. drama countering naturalism: dreamlike, mythic, emphasizing inner mood and spiritual truth over external accuracy. Anti-realist register.
Exemplars: Maurice Maeterlinck's Pelleas and Melisande; Yeats's plays; Strindberg's A Dream Play (variant); some Synge.

## 489. Expressionist Drama
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Distorted setting reflecting interior
2. Fragmented dialogue
3. Visions or hallucinations integral
4. Crisis as subjective experience
5. Climactic vision
6. Closing on subjective truth
Description: Early-20th-c. drama foregrounding subjective psychological experience — distorted reality, fragmented dialogue, inner truth made visible. Specifically Weimar / German register.
Exemplars: Georg Kaiser; Ernst Toller; Eugene O'Neill's The Emperor Jones; Elmer Rice's The Adding Machine.

## 490. Theatre of the Absurd
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Empty or symbolic setting
2. Characters without coherent backstory
3. Repetitive, collapsing dialogue
4. Apparent waiting for meaning that doesn't come
5. Climactic absurd moment
6. No resolution — repetition implied
Description: Mid-20th-c. drama in which existential meaninglessness is staged through breakdown of language, narrative coherence, and character logic. Post-WWII philosophical register.
Exemplars: Beckett's Waiting for Godot and Endgame; Ionesco's The Bald Soprano; Edward Albee's The Zoo Story; Harold Pinter's early plays.

## 491. Epic Theatre (Brechtian)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Direct address to audience
2. Episodic scenes with titles
3. Songs that interrupt
4. Characters present positions, not psychologies
5. Climactic political question posed
6. Audience provoked to think rather than feel
Description: Bertolt Brecht's theatrical method: explicit anti-illusionism, Verfremdungseffekt (alienation effect), political analysis, episodic structure. Refusal of emotional immersion.
Exemplars: Brecht's The Threepenny Opera, Mother Courage, The Caucasian Chalk Circle; Tony Kushner's Angels in America (Brechtian variant).

## 492. Theatre of Cruelty (Artaud)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Sensory total environment
2. Ritual rather than narrative structure
3. Bodies, sounds, lights as primary
4. Confrontation with the audience
5. Cathartic ritual climax
6. Audience transformed (or attacked)
Description: Antonin Artaud's theatrical theory and practice: theater as ritual, sensory assault, transformative collective experience. Theater that wounds and heals.
Exemplars: Artaud's The Cenci; later Jerzy Grotowski; Peter Brook's Marat/Sade; Living Theatre productions.

## 493. Postdramatic Theatre
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Performance-event begins (not necessarily a play)
2. No clear narrative or character development
3. Image, sound, body as primary materials
4. Audience as participants in event
5. Climactic moment of performance presence
6. Event ends
Description: Late-20th/early-21st-c. theatrical mode that abandons traditional dramatic structure — text, plot, character — in favor of performance, image, body, and event. Post-textual ambition.
Exemplars: Robert Wilson's productions; Heiner Müller's late works; Forced Entertainment; The Wooster Group; Hans-Thies Lehmann's theorization.

## 494. Dramatic Monologue
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Speaker addresses listener (silent or off-stage)
2. Establishes situation
3. Self-presentation reveals more than intended
4. Tension between what's said and what's meant
5. Climactic self-revelation
6. Speech ends, often with silence
Description: Dramatic form (in theater or poetry) consisting of a single character's extended speech to implied or silent listener. Character's psychology emerges through what they reveal and conceal.
Exemplars: Robert Browning's "My Last Duchess"; Beckett's Krapp's Last Tape; Eric Bogosian's monologue plays; Talking Heads (Alan Bennett).

## 495. Duologue (Two-Hander)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Two characters introduced together
2. Initial dynamic established
3. Tension grows through dialogue alone
4. Backstory revealed in exchange
5. Climactic confrontation
6. New equilibrium between the two
Description: Drama for exactly two characters — sustained dialogue, intimate scope, no external relief. Tight structural constraint.
Exemplars: Edward Albee's The Zoo Story and Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (variant); Pinter's The Dumb Waiter; Beckett's Krapp (one-hander variant).

## 496. One-Person Show
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Performer enters and addresses audience
2. Establishes identity (own or character's)
3. Multiple voices/perspectives sometimes performed
4. Building emotional or thematic arc
5. Climactic moment of revelation or confrontation
6. Final direct connection with audience
Description: Theatrical form featuring a single performer for the entire show — autobiographical, character-impersonation, or political. Single-performer constraint.
Exemplars: Spalding Gray's monologues; Anna Deavere Smith's Twilight: Los Angeles; Eric Bogosian; Hannah Gadsby's Nanette.

## 497. Devised Theatre
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Initial concept or research material
2. Ensemble exploration through improvisation
3. Material discovered through bodies
4. Provisional structure shaped
5. Performance integrating fixed and improvised
6. Experience that resists fixed text
Description: Theatre created collaboratively by an ensemble through workshops and improvisation, often without a pre-existing script. Collective-creation method.
Exemplars: Forced Entertainment; Complicité; Punchdrunk's Sleep No More; Mike Leigh's films (devised process).

## 498. Immersive Theatre
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Audience enters constructed environment
2. Wanders or follows individual actor
3. Encounters scenes simultaneously occurring
4. Personal experience shaped by choices
5. Climactic shared moment
6. Audience exits with incomplete-but-personal version
Description: Theatre that abandons the audience-stage division — audience moves through environments, encounters performers individually, makes choices that affect what they see.
Exemplars: Punchdrunk's Sleep No More; Then She Fell; Felix Barrett productions; some Robert Wilson.

## 499. Documentary Theatre / Verbatim
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Real-world event or community as subject
2. Sources interviewed and documented
3. Verbatim text edited
4. Actors perform real people
5. Multiple perspectives presented
6. Audience encounters reality at theatrical distance
Description: Theatrical work whose text is drawn entirely from real-world sources — interviews, court transcripts, news reports — performed by actors. Verbatim documentary commitment.
Exemplars: Anna Deavere Smith's verbatim plays; The Laramie Project (Tectonic Theater Project); David Hare's Stuff Happens; Tricycle Theatre tribunal plays.

## 500. Play with Songs
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Theatrical scene establishes situation
2. Song expresses what dialogue cannot
3. Scene resumes after song
4. Action progresses through scene-song-scene structure
5. Climactic song or moment
6. Theatrical resolution
Description: Theatrical form combining spoken text with songs, distinct from full musical by less integrated song-structure. More theatrical-than-commercial commitment.
Exemplars: Brecht-Weill's The Threepenny Opera (proto); Michael John LaChiusa; Adam Guettel's The Light in the Piazza; Sondheim's more theatrical works.
