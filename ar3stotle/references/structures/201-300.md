# Structures 201-300

## 201. Transfer
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Loaded symbol introduced
2. Proposition placed alongside it
3. Audience transfers feeling
Description: Associating the proposition with a symbol, image, or institution that the audience already values (flag, religion, family) — transferring positive feeling.
Exemplars: Patriotic political ads; brand storytelling that invokes religious imagery; nostalgic marketing.

## 202. Name Calling
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opposition introduced
2. Pejorative label applied
3. Audience encouraged to dismiss without examining
Description: Discrediting the opposing position by attaching pejorative labels — without engaging the substance. Recognized propaganda technique; ethically loaded.
Exemplars: Political mudslinging; ad hominem attacks; ideological labeling.

## 203. Fear Appeal
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Threat established
2. Consequences vivid
3. Solution offered (the proposition)
4. Action urged before fear subsides
Description: Persuasion grounded in arousing fear of consequences if audience does not adopt the position. Powerful and ethically heavy.
Exemplars: Public-health campaigns (smoking, drunk driving); insurance ads; political fear campaigns.

## 204. Editorial (institutional voice)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Issue defined
2. Editorial board's position
3. Reasoning and evidence
4. Specific call to action
5. Closing institutional declaration
Description: Unsigned argumentative piece representing the position of a publication's editorial board. Distinguished from op-eds by institutional weight.
Exemplars: New York Times editorials; Wall Street Journal editorials; The Economist leaders.

## 205. Letter to the Editor
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Reference to original article
2. Position
3. One or two pointed reasons
4. Closing return to the larger stake
Description: Short, signed reader response to previous coverage. 200-400 words. Conversational claim on shared readership.
Exemplars: Daily newspaper letters pages; London Review of Books letters; New York Review of Books letters.

## 206. Obituary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Death announcement (with cause and date)
2. Life's distinctive achievement
3. Background and rise
4. Defining episodes
5. Survivors and legacy
Description: Biographical narrative of a recently-deceased person. Valedictory mode — what was this life for.
Exemplars: New York Times obituaries; The Economist's Monday obit; Telegraph's eccentric obituaries.

## 207. Sports Feature
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Vivid game or athlete moment
2. Athlete's backstory
3. Stakes of the present moment
4. Defining play or decision
5. What the result means beyond sport
Description: Long-form sports narrative going beyond game results to character, context, and meaning. The sports event or athlete carries broader human or cultural weight.
Exemplars: Sports Illustrated features; The Players' Tribune; W.C. Heinz; Gary Smith.

## 208. Service Journalism / How-to
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Reader's problem named
2. Stakes acknowledged
3. Steps with rationale
4. Pitfalls and variations
5. Outcome described
Description: Practical instructional content presented in journalistic form. Blends process analysis with editorial selection.
Exemplars: Wirecutter product guides; Lifehacker how-tos; women's-magazine service features.

## 209. News Analysis
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Recent event described
2. Context and history given
3. Stakeholders' positions
4. Likely consequences
5. What to watch next
Description: Signed journalist's interpretation of news events — distinguishing implications, providing context, identifying stakes. Distinct from news (reports) and op-ed (argues): analysis explains and frames.
Exemplars: NYT "News Analysis" pieces; Politico analysis pieces; Atlantic explanatory journalism.

## 210. Listicle
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hook (the count)
2. Item 1 with detail
3. Item 2
4. Successive items
5. Closing image or kicker
Description: Information presented as a numbered list with brief descriptions. Scannable structure: each item is self-contained.
Exemplars: BuzzFeed lists; Cracked features; many magazine year-end lists.

## 211. Annual Letter to Shareholders
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Year's headline result
2. Strategic context
3. What worked and what didn't
4. Lessons
5. Forward-looking vision
6. Personal closing
Description: CEO's signed reflection on the year's performance, strategy, and vision. Hybrid: financial accountability + brand storytelling + strategic communication.
Exemplars: Warren Buffett's Berkshire letters; Jeff Bezos's Amazon shareholder letters; Howard Schultz's Starbucks letters.

## 212. Quarterly Earnings Call narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. CEO opens with strategic frame
2. CFO walks numbers
3. Highlights and challenges acknowledged
4. Forward guidance
5. Q&A
6. Closing summary
Description: Structured corporate narrative delivered to analysts and investors quarterly. Engineered for credibility under scrutiny.
Exemplars: Apple, Microsoft, Tesla earnings calls; SEC-required communications.

## 213. SWOT-Style Strategic Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Strategic question framed
2. Strengths
3. Weaknesses
4. Opportunities
5. Threats
6. Synthesis with recommended direction
Description: Strategic analysis organized as Strengths / Weaknesses / Opportunities / Threats — internal and external, positive and negative.
Exemplars: Business school cases; consulting deliverables; corporate strategy reviews.

## 214. OKR Story
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Objective stated
2. Why it matters
3. Key results defined
4. Progress against each KR
5. Lessons
6. Next-cycle objectives
Description: Strategic narrative organized around Objectives (qualitative goals) and Key Results (quantitative measures). Used at Intel, Google, and across modern tech.
Exemplars: Google internal reviews; modern tech company quarterly reviews; Measure What Matters (Doerr).

## 215. Post-Mortem / Retrospective
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Incident summary
2. Timeline of events
3. Root cause analysis
4. Contributing factors
5. What went well
6. Action items for future
Description: Structured analysis of a project, incident, or failure after the fact, with the explicit goal of organizational learning rather than blame.
Exemplars: Engineering post-mortems (Google SRE tradition); aviation incident reports; agile retrospectives.

## 216. Conference Paper / Talk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hook with question or stakes
2. Background and prior work
3. Methodology
4. Results
5. Implications
6. Closing with call for questions
Description: Short academic presentation (typically 15-20 minutes) at a conference. Compressed argument oriented toward in-room peer scrutiny.
Exemplars: Most academic conference talks; AAAS, AMA, MLA conferences; academic colloquia.

## 217. Dissertation Defense
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Research question
2. Theoretical framework
3. Methodology
4. Findings
5. Contributions
6. Limitations
7. Future research
8. Q&A
Description: Doctoral candidate's oral defense of their dissertation before a committee. High-stakes mastery demonstration under expert challenge.
Exemplars: PhD dissertation defenses across disciplines; standard academic credentialing.

## 218. Academic Book Review
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Book and field situated
2. Argument summarized fairly
3. Strengths assessed
4. Weaknesses identified
5. Place in disciplinary conversation
6. Recommendation
Description: Scholarly review of an academic book, situating it within a field and assessing contributions. Emphasizes disciplinary conversation.
Exemplars: American Historical Review book reviews; Journal of Philosophy reviews; field-specific academic journals.

## 219. Creation myth (cosmogony)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Primordial state (chaos, void, primal waters)
2. First differentiation (light/dark, sky/earth)
3. Emergence of cosmic order
4. Creation of beings (gods, humans)
5. Establishment of fundamental relationships
Description: Narrative explaining how the world, universe, or fundamental order came to be. Establishes the most basic terms of reality.
Exemplars: Genesis 1-2; Enuma Elish (Babylonian); Hesiod's Theogony; Diné creation; Yoruba Olódùmarè narratives.

## 220. Flood myth
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Humanity in moral decline
2. Divine decision to destroy
3. Chosen survivor warned
4. Preparations made (ark, vessel, mountain)
5. Flood arrives
6. Survivors emerge
7. Covenant or new beginning established
Description: Widely-distributed narrative type. Divine displeasure leads to a great flood; chosen few survive to rebuild. Found in Mesopotamian, Hebrew, Greek, Hindu, Aztec traditions.
Exemplars: Genesis 6-9 (Noah); Epic of Gilgamesh (Utnapishtim); Greek Deucalion; Hindu Manu; Aztec Coxcoxtli.

## 221. Theogony
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Primordial divine principle
2. First gods emerge
3. Generation of children gods
4. Conflict between generations (often: child overthrows parent)
5. Establishment of current divine order
6. Distribution of cosmic offices
Description: Narrative of the origin and genealogy of the gods. Distinguished from cosmogony by its specific concern with how the divine pantheon came into being and stratified.
Exemplars: Hesiod's Theogony; Norse Eddic god-genealogies; Hurrian-Hittite Kumarbi cycle; Sumerian Enuma Elish.

## 222. Hero-Birth narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Distinguished parents
2. Difficult conception or birth (often divine)
3. Prophecy of greatness or threat to authority
4. Authority's attempt to destroy infant
5. Exposure/abandonment
6. Rescue and raising elsewhere
7. Eventual recognition and accession
Description: Otto Rank's identified pattern. Hero's birth involves miraculous circumstances, attempt to destroy infant, exposure, rescue and raising by humble or animal foster-parents, eventual recognition.
Exemplars: Moses; Romulus and Remus; Oedipus; Krishna; Sargon of Akkad; Cyrus the Great.

## 223. Trickster Origin Cycle
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Trickster's appetite or desire
2. Scheme launched
3. Violation of cosmic norm
4. Consequence as world-feature
5. Continuation onward to next adventure
Description: Pan-cultural narrative pattern. Trickster figure (Coyote, Anansi, Loki, Maui, Hermes, Eshu) wanders early world breaking rules; byproduct of misadventures is origin of present-world features.
Exemplars: Anansi tales (Akan); Coyote tales (Plains/Southwest); Maui tales (Polynesian); Loki tales (Norse); Hermes (Greek).

## 224. Apocalyptic / End-Times narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Signs of the coming end
2. Witness or prophet recognizes
3. Forces of dissolution gather
4. Final battle or judgment
5. Old order destroyed
6. New order or rebirth (in some traditions)
Description: Narrative of the foretold or imminent end of an era, world, or order — often involving cosmic battle, judgment, and renewal. Ultimate stakes; structural payoff is reckoning.
Exemplars: Book of Revelation; Norse Ragnarök; Hindu Kali Yuga / Kalki; Aztec Five Suns; Daniel and 2 Esdras.

## 225. Anecdote
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setup — when and where
2. Single incident
3. Punctuating moment or insight
Description: Short narrative of a single incident, usually personal, recounted to make a point, illustrate a character, or generate laughter. The shortest narrative form.
Exemplars: Conversational storytelling; speeches that hinge on a single illustrating tale; many social-media micro-stories.

## 226. Joke (3-beat)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setup — establishes premise
2. Build — second iteration that confirms expectation
3. Punchline — third iteration that subverts expectation
Description: Setup, escalation, punchline. The classical compressed comedy form. Use when laughter is the entire purpose and structural economy is key.
Exemplars: "A priest, a rabbi, and a minister walk into a bar..."; standup comedy; rule-of-three jokes.

## 227. Tall Tale
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Plausible opening
2. First exaggeration
3. Each successive claim larger
4. Climactic impossibility
5. Deadpan close
Description: Story whose comedy comes from progressive exaggeration — each detail more impossibly large than the last. Often featuring legendary folk heroes (Paul Bunyan, Pecos Bill).
Exemplars: Paul Bunyan tales; Pecos Bill; American frontier tall tales; Mark Twain's Roughing It.

## 228. Urban Legend
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. "This happened to a friend of a friend"
2. Ordinary situation established
3. Strange or threatening element introduced
4. Twist or revelation
5. Cautionary moral implied
Description: Modern folk-narrative presented as having happened to a friend-of-a-friend, typically with horror, ironic, or cautionary twist. Perpetual "almost-true" claim.
Exemplars: "The Hook"; "The Vanishing Hitchhiker"; "Bloody Mary"; collected by Jan Harold Brunvand.

## 229. Cautionary Tale (general)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist tempted toward forbidden choice
2. Warning given (often ignored)
3. Choice made
4. Consequence falls
5. Moral made explicit
Description: Narrative whose primary function is warning — depicting a character's poor choice and consequences, so audience may avoid the same fate. Moral instruction through negative example.
Exemplars: Little Red Riding Hood; Pinocchio; Reefer Madness; Requiem for a Dream.

## 230. Riddle-Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Riddle posed (often with stakes)
2. Failed attempts at solution
3. Insight or trickery
4. Solution revealed
5. Consequences of solving (or failing)
Description: Narrative organized around the posing of a riddle whose solution is the climax. Reader/listener invited to solve along with characters. Intellectual play as the structural pleasure.
Exemplars: Oedipus and the Sphinx; Bilbo and Gollum; Anglo-Saxon Exeter Book riddles; Turandot.

## 231. Proverb-Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Situation that will exemplify the proverb
2. Action illustrating its truth
3. Outcome
4. Proverb stated
Description: Short narrative whose closing line is a proverb or saying that crystallizes the tale's meaning. The story exists to ground and authenticate the proverb. Condensed wisdom validated by example.
Exemplars: Aesop's fables; West African Akan proverb stories; Yoruba proverb-narratives; Chinese chengyu origin tales.

## 232. Sermon
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Scripture read and contextualized
2. Exegetical exposition
3. Theological point developed
4. Application to listener's life
5. Closing exhortation or call
Description: Religious address — typically based on scripture — combining exegesis, moral exhortation, and pastoral application. Interpretive spiritual instruction with explicit application.
Exemplars: Augustine's sermons; Jonathan Edwards's "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"; MLK sermons; Islamic khutbah; Jewish drash.

## 233. Parable
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ordinary scene established (sower, vineyard, household)
2. Decisive incident or choice
3. Outcome that exceeds or inverts expectation
4. Spiritual application offered or implied
Description: Short illustrative story in which surface narrative carries spiritual or moral teaching. Distinguished from fable by human characters and religious context. Spiritual truth approached obliquely.
Exemplars: Parables of Jesus (Good Samaritan, Prodigal Son, Sower); rabbinic mashal; Sufi parables (Rumi, Attar); Buddhist parables.

## 234. Prayer
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Invocation (naming the divine)
2. Acknowledgment of divine attribute
3. Confession or thanksgiving
4. Petition
5. Closing affirmation ("Amen," "Inshallah")
Description: Direct address to the divine, usually with structured movement: invocation, acknowledgment, petition, thanksgiving, closing. Speaker-to-divine address across traditions.
Exemplars: The Lord's Prayer; Islamic Salah; Hindu Sandhyavandanam; Buddhist refuge formulas; secular prayer-shaped meditations.

## 235. Confession narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Speaker's pre-failure self
2. Sin or failure described
3. Recognition of wrongness
4. Confession spoken
5. Plea for forgiveness or change
Description: First-person narrative of moral failure and acknowledgment, often within a religious frame seeking forgiveness. Honest accounting before God or community.
Exemplars: Augustine's Confessions; Tolstoy's A Confession; Rousseau's Confessions; AA / 12-step testimonies.

## 236. Conversion testimony
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-conversion life and its dissatisfactions
2. Crisis or encounter
3. Moment of decision or revelation
4. Visible signs of new life
5. Closing testimony to others
Description: First-person narrative of religious conversion. Pre-conversion life, awakening or crisis, decision, transformed post-conversion life. The moment of changed allegiance.
Exemplars: Paul's Damascus account (Acts 9); John Newton's "Amazing Grace" backstory; Augustine's Confessions; modern evangelical testimony.

## 237. Hagiography (saint's life)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Birth and early signs of holiness
2. Early life and calling
3. Trials and miracles
4. Spiritual mastery achieved
5. Death (often with miraculous accompaniment)
6. Posthumous miracles and devotion
Description: Biographical narrative of a holy figure — saint, mystic, religious founder — emphasizing miraculous events, exemplary virtue, and posthumous influence. Devotional register.
Exemplars: Athanasius's Life of Anthony; Sufi tadhkirat al-awliya (Attar); Hindu Bhakti hagiographies; Catholic saints' lives.

## 238. Mystical Vision narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mystic's prior state
2. Onset of the experience (often unexpected)
3. The experience itself (rendered in the limits of language)
4. Return to ordinary consciousness
5. Reflection on what was seen and what cannot be said
Description: First-person account of direct religious experience — vision, ecstasy, union with the divine. Centered on the experience itself, not its conditions.
Exemplars: Teresa of Ávila's Interior Castle; Julian of Norwich's Revelations; Cloud of Unknowing; Rumi's ecstatic poems; Hildegard of Bingen.

## 239. Eulogy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledgment of the gathering and shared loss
2. Brief life-and-character portrait
3. Specific stories that embody the deceased
4. What we lose and what they leave
5. Closing benediction or farewell
Description: Speech delivered in honor of a recently-deceased person, typically at the funeral. Holds collective grief while honoring the individual.
Exemplars: Pericles's funeral oration (Thucydides); famous eulogies in modern public life; family eulogies generally.

## 240. Toast
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Greeting and acknowledgment of the moment
2. Brief story or reflection on the honoree
3. The honoree's distinctive quality
4. Wish for their future
5. Raising of the glass — "To [honoree]"
Description: Short speech offered at a celebratory gathering, raising a glass in honor of a person, occasion, or shared value. Brevity, public address, ceremonial close.
Exemplars: Wedding toasts; retirement toasts; New Year's Eve toasts; many cultural toast traditions.

## 241. Wedding Speech (Best Man / Maid of Honor)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Greeting and brief self-introduction
2. How the speaker knows the couple
3. Funny story or affectionate roast
4. Sincere observation about the couple's bond
5. Wish for their future
6. Toast
Description: Toast-extension delivered at a wedding by a chosen friend. Mixed tonal register: comedy, sentiment, ceremonial witnessing all in one.
Exemplars: Wedding speech traditions across cultures; comedy films featuring famous wedding-speech scenes (Four Weddings and a Funeral).

## 242. Roast
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setup — speaker positioned as "rougher" than usual
2. Series of mock-insults
3. Each insult escalates in pointedness
4. Pivot to genuine affection
5. Closing acknowledgment of the bond
Description: Comic ceremonial speech where a person is teased through pointed insults understood as affectionate. The ceremony's structure protects the honoree; affection is underneath the cruelty.
Exemplars: Friars Club roasts; Comedy Central roasts; Dean Martin's celebrity roasts; modern televised roast formats.

## 243. Acceptance Speech
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Expression of surprise or thanks
2. Acknowledgment of the awarding body
3. Specific people thanked
4. Brief reflection on what the work means
5. Closing — often a call or a wish
Description: Speech delivered upon receiving an award or honor. Hybrid: gratitude + brief vision + acknowledgment of others. Engineered for brevity under emotional pressure.
Exemplars: Oscar acceptance speeches; Nobel Prize lectures; honorary doctorate addresses.

## 244. Commencement Address
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledgment of the moment
2. Personal story or memory from the speaker's own life at this stage
3. Lessons learned (often three)
4. Direct address to graduates' fears and hopes
5. Closing benediction or charge
Description: Speech delivered at graduation ceremony, typically by outside speaker. Engineered for an audience facing transition. Moves from acknowledging the moment to offering wisdom for what comes next.
Exemplars: David Foster Wallace's "This Is Water"; Steve Jobs's Stanford 2005 address; J.K. Rowling at Harvard; Toni Morrison's commencement addresses.

## 245. Social Realism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Working-class community established
2. Pressures of economic / political structure shown
3. Individual struggle within structure
4. Costs of structure named
5. Implicit or explicit call for change
Description: Fiction depicting working-class life, social conditions, and economic structures, typically with reformist intent. Politically-reformist register.
Exemplars: Steinbeck's The Grapes of Wrath; Sinclair's The Jungle; Richard Wright's Native Son; Sembène's films and novels.

## 246. Naturalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Character's inherited and environmental conditions established
2. Aspirations or efforts to transcend
3. Forces close in
4. Inevitable outcome (often degradation or death)
5. Closing acknowledgment of the deterministic frame
Description: Fiction grounded in assumption that human behavior is determined by environment, heredity, and circumstance. Quasi-scientific determinism.
Exemplars: Zola's Rougon-Macquart cycle; Crane's Maggie: A Girl of the Streets; Dreiser's Sister Carrie; Frank Norris's McTeague.

## 247. Kitchen-Sink Realism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Working-class home in industrial setting
2. Daily routine rendered
3. Domestic conflict surfaces
4. Resolution that changes nothing structural
5. Continuation of the daily texture
Description: Mid-20th-c. British genre depicting working-class domestic life with unflinching ordinariness, typically in industrial-Northern settings. Granular texture of working-class home and pub.
Exemplars: Osborne's Look Back in Anger; Sillitoe's Saturday Night and Sunday Morning; Mike Leigh's films; Ken Loach's films.

## 248. Dirty Realism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Diminished circumstance established without comment
2. Small action with large emotional weight
3. Telling detail accumulates
4. Quiet turn or non-resolution
5. Closing image
Description: Late-20th-c. American minimalist fiction depicting the underside of American life — trailer parks, motels, ordinary failures — with stripped-down prose and unsentimental detail.
Exemplars: Carver's What We Talk About When We Talk About Love; Richard Ford's Rock Springs; Tobias Wolff; Bobbie Ann Mason.

## 249. Greek Tragedy (proper Aristotelian)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Prologue sets situation
2. Parodos (chorus enters)
3. Episodes of escalating crisis
4. Stasima (chorus's reflective songs between episodes)
5. Anagnorisis (recognition) and peripeteia (reversal)
6. Catastrophe — protagonist's fall
7. Exodos — final chorus
Description: Classical Athenian drama as theorized by Aristotle: noble protagonist's reversal through hamartia, evoking pity and fear, achieving catharsis. Fated structure with choral commentary.
Exemplars: Sophocles' Oedipus Rex; Euripides' Medea; Aeschylus' Oresteia; Sophocles' Antigone.

## 250. Senecan Tragedy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Exposition with rhetorical declamation
2. Rising action with ghost or supernatural figure
3. Climax with rhetorical heightening
4. Falling action with on-stage violence
5. Catastrophe with macabre intensity
Description: Roman tragedy in style of Seneca — five acts, rhetorical heightening, ghosts, on-stage violence, declamation over action. Closet-drama orientation. Heightened rhetoric and macabre intensity.
Exemplars: Seneca's Thyestes, Medea, Phaedra; influenced Renaissance Revenge Tragedy.

## 251. Revenge Tragedy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Murder discovered or revealed (often by ghost)
2. Revenger's commitment to vengeance
3. Plotting and feigned madness
4. Initial revenge actions
5. Moral compromise of the revenger
6. Climactic bloodbath
7. All major figures dead
Description: Renaissance English subgenre. Protagonist seeks revenge for kinsman's murder, undergoes moral degradation, dies with antagonists. Moral consumption through revenge.
Exemplars: Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy; Shakespeare's Hamlet; Webster's The Duchess of Malfi; Tourneur's The Revenger's Tragedy.

## 252. Domestic Tragedy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Domestic situation established
2. Pressure or temptation enters
3. Family member yields or fails
4. Consequences fall on the family
5. Final loss or destruction
Description: Tragedy set in middle- or working-class domestic life rather than royal settings. Democratization of tragic stature. Ordinary life under tragic pressure.
Exemplars: Miller's Death of a Salesman; O'Neill's Long Day's Journey Into Night; Williams's A Streetcar Named Desire; Ibsen's A Doll's House.

## 253. Comedy of Manners
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Social setting and its conventions established
2. Pretensions and affectations exhibited
3. Misunderstanding or mismatched expectations
4. Witty exchanges expose the gap
5. Affectations punctured (without being destroyed)
Description: Satirical comedy that mocks the affectations, etiquette, and pretensions of a specific social class. The gap between manners and reality.
Exemplars: Congreve's The Way of the World; Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest; Noel Coward's plays; Jane Austen's novels.

## 254. Farce
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent setup
2. First mistaken identity or unlikely circumstance
3. Stakes and confusion compound
4. Chase, hide, mistaken-identity escalation
5. Climactic moment of total chaos
6. Resolution — often by exhaustion rather than logic
Description: Comedy built on improbable situations, mistaken identities, slamming doors, and accelerating chaos. Physical and situational comedy as escalating impossibility.
Exemplars: Feydeau's farces; Frayn's Noises Off; A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum; Marx Brothers films.

## 255. Satire
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Target's pretensions established
2. Exaggeration that exposes
3. Comparison with absurd parallel
4. Pretension destroyed by its own logic
5. Implicit moral revealed
Description: Comedy with critical intent — exposing folly, hypocrisy, or vice through ridicule. Two main modes: Horatian (gentle) and Juvenalian (savage). Moral-political target.
Exemplars: Swift's A Modest Proposal; Gulliver's Travels; Catch-22; Dr. Strangelove; Animal Farm.

## 256. Black / Dark Comedy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Grim or taboo situation established
2. Treated with comic register
3. Audience's discomfort and laughter both engaged
4. Escalation — situation gets worse and funnier
5. Closing — often without redemptive moral
Description: Comedy that finds humor in subjects normally treated with seriousness or horror — death, disease, war, atrocity. Uncomfortable laughter; grim reality is funny.
Exemplars: Dr. Strangelove; MASH; Heller's Catch-22; The Death of Stalin; Fargo; Martin McDonagh's plays.

## 257. Slapstick
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent character in normal situation
2. First physical mishap
3. Escalating physical chaos
4. Climactic large physical event
5. Relief or recovery
Description: Physical comedy based on broad, exaggerated, body-based humor — pratfalls, pies in faces, chase sequences. Pure body-driven, language-free comedy.
Exemplars: Charlie Chaplin's films; Buster Keaton; Three Stooges; Looney Tunes; Mr. Bean.

## 258. Parody
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Target genre or work invoked through stylistic imitation
2. Conventions exaggerated or made visible
3. Pleasure of recognition for the audience
4. Climactic over-fulfillment of the convention
5. Closing wink
Description: Comedy that imitates the style and conventions of a specific work or genre to comic effect. The formal target is what's being mocked.
Exemplars: Mel Brooks's Young Frankenstein, Spaceballs; Airplane!; This Is Spinal Tap; Galaxy Quest; Cervantes's Don Quixote.

## 259. Opera narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Overture sets emotional palette
2. Act 1 — situation, principals introduced through arias
3. Conflict and intrigue develop
4. Act 2-3 — crisis, betrayal, climactic arias
5. Final act — death, reunion, or transformation
6. Closing chorus
Description: Sung-through dramatic narrative combining music, poetry, and theater. 3-4 acts with arias for character interiority, ensembles for conflict. Heightened emotion sustainable only through music.
Exemplars: Verdi's La Traviata; Puccini's La Bohème; Mozart's Don Giovanni; Wagner's Ring Cycle; Adams's Nixon in China.

## 260. Broadway Musical
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. "I Want" song — protagonist's desire stated
2. Inciting event in spoken scene
3. Series of book scenes alternating with songs
4. Act 1 close — usually a high-energy ensemble
5. Act 2 — complications and 11 o'clock number
6. Resolution and finale
Description: American musical-theater form combining book (spoken scenes), songs, and dance. English vernacular and integrated book-songs structure.
Exemplars: West Side Story; Hamilton; Les Misérables; Chicago; Sweeney Todd; Wicked.

## 261. Ballet narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setting established through ensemble
2. Principal characters introduced through pas de deux
3. Complication or magical event
4. Variation pieces explore character
5. Climactic confrontation or transformation
6. Resolution through grand ensemble
Description: Narrative dance work, typically without text, communicating story through choreography, music, costume, and staging. Body-narrative — story through bodies in motion.
Exemplars: Tchaikovsky's Swan Lake, The Nutcracker, Sleeping Beauty; Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring; Giselle.

## 262. Testimonial Ad
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Customer introduced
2. Pre-product problem
3. Discovery of product
4. Use
5. Outcome
6. Closing recommendation
Description: Advertising in which a real (or plausible) customer narrates their experience with the product. Peer credibility rather than authority endorsement.
Exemplars: Most consumer-product advertising; "real people, not actors" campaigns; modern Instagram ad creator content.

## 263. Comparison Ad
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Competitor named or implied
2. Side-by-side comparison
3. Key advantage demonstrated
4. Conclusion in product's favor
Description: Advertising that explicitly compares the product to a competitor's, highlighting advantages. Named adversarial structure.
Exemplars: Apple's "Mac vs. PC" campaign; Pepsi Challenge; Tesla's silent-comparison-with-gas-cars marketing.

## 264. Lifestyle Ad
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Aspirational scene established
2. Subject living the lifestyle
3. Product as natural part of the scene
4. Implication: this is who you could be
Description: Advertising that sells the product through association with an aspirational way of life rather than functional features. Identity-coded value.
Exemplars: Ralph Lauren campaigns; Apple's "Shot on iPhone"; Patagonia's outdoor narratives; luxury-goods advertising.

## 265. Before / After Ad
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Before state — pain, lack, problem
2. Discovery of product
3. Use
4. After state — pleasure, fulfillment, solution
5. Call to action
Description: Advertising that shows the subject's state before using the product and after, with the product as transformative agent. Direct, conversion-focused.
Exemplars: Weight-loss ads; home-renovation shows; before-and-after marketing across industries.

## 266. Product Launch Story
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Problem in the world
2. Insight that produced solution
3. Demonstration of product
4. Stories of early use
5. Implications for the future
6. Call to acquire
Description: Narrative announcing a new product, woven around the problem the product solves, the insight that produced it, and the future it makes possible.
Exemplars: Apple keynote launches (Steve Jobs's iPhone, 2007); Tesla unveilings; SaaS-product launch posts.

## 267. Filibuster narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Procedural opening
2. Substantive content (often reading from prepared materials)
3. Personal anecdote
4. Address to fellow members
5. Continued speaking
6. Eventual yielding or vote
Description: Extended speech delivered to delay legislative action — the act of speaking is itself the political move. Hybrid: substantive content + procedural function.
Exemplars: Strom Thurmond's 24-hour filibuster (1957); Wendy Davis's Texas filibuster (2013); Bernie Sanders 2010; Mr. Smith Goes to Washington.

## 268. Town Hall Address
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Greeting and acknowledgment of community
2. Brief remarks
3. Open Q&A
4. Direct response to specific questions
5. Closing thanks
Description: Politician's direct engagement with constituents, structured as remarks followed by Q&A. Semi-improvised middle and accountability-under-pressure tenor.
Exemplars: Modern American town halls; British MP constituency surgeries; presidential town hall debates.

## 269. Debate format
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening statements
2. Question 1 with each side's response
3. Rebuttals
4. Successive questions
5. Closing statements
Description: Structured public confrontation between candidates or positions, governed by formal rules of time, response, and rebuttal. Contest visible to voters.
Exemplars: Lincoln-Douglas debates (1858); Kennedy-Nixon (1960); modern presidential debates; British Question Time.

## 270. Pilgrimage Memoir

3. Stages of the journey, each with a lesson
4. Crisis or doubt
5. Arrival at the sacred destination
6. Encounter with the holy
7. Return home, transformed
Description: First-person narrative of a religious or spiritual pilgrimage — Mecca, Jerusalem, Camino, Varanasi, Mount Kailash. The journey is interior as well as exterior.
Exemplars: Bashō's Oku no Hosomichi; Cheryl Strayed's Wild (secular); The Way of a Pilgrim; Robert Macfarlane's The Old Ways.

## 271. Spiritual Autobiography
Tradition: Universal
Beats:

2. Awakening or first calling
3. Trials of faith
4. Crisis or dark night
5. Mature faith
6. Reflections on lifelong learning
Description: Long-form first-person account of a life's spiritual development. Distinct from secular autobiography by emphasis on the soul's journey.
Exemplars: Augustine's Confessions; Teresa of Ávila's Life; Merton's The Seven Storey Mountain; Yogananda's The Autobiography of a Yogi; C.S. Lewis's Surprised by Joy.

## 272. Allegorical Religious Narrative

Beats:

3. Each encounter represents a spiritual challenge
4. Trials map onto soul-stages
5. Arrival at allegorical destination
6. Allegorical key revealed (sometimes)
Description: Narrative whose surface story figures a deeper spiritual journey — characters, places, and events stand for spiritual states or lessons. Spiritual content encountered through story rather than argument.
Exemplars: Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress; Everyman; Spenser's The Faerie Queene; Hesse's Siddhartha; Coelho's The Alchemist.

4. Main content delivered in 2-4 sub-sections
5. Examples and applications
6. Synthesis
7. Q&A
Description: Structured oral presentation of a topic by an expert to learners, typically 30-60 minutes. Pedagogical (not peer-review) audience.

Beats:

4. Worked examples
5. Application problems

Description: Structured educational chapter combining exposition, examples, and review questions. Engineered for systematic curriculum coverage.

## 276. TED Talk format
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hook (often a personal story)
2. Big idea introduced
3. Evidence and examples
4. Implications
5. Closing call or vision
6. A single image to remember
Description: Short (5-18 minute) talk format engineered for memorability and viral sharing. Combines personal story, big idea, and call to action. One big idea worth spreading.

Beats:
1. Concept stated

3. Worked example with narration
4. Variation or related example
5. Common mistakes flagged
6. Practice prompt
Description: Short, screen-captured video lesson explaining a single concept with examples — common in modern online education. Specific compressed-video format.

## 278. MOOC Module

Beats:
1. Module learning objectives
2. Video lectures
3. Required readings
4. Discussion forum prompts
5. Quiz or assignment
6. Module wrap-up
Description: Self-paced online course unit combining video lecture, readings, quizzes, and discussion. Asynchronous remote-pedagogy structure.
Exemplars: Coursera, edX, Udacity courses; Stanford Online; MITx.

Beats:

## 280. Apprenticeship / Master Class

Beats:

## 281. Hard Science Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Scientific premise established with specificity
2. Implications systematically extrapolated
3. Characters confront the consequences
4. Conflict turns on understanding the science correctly
5. Resolution consistent with the premise
Description: SF grounded in scientifically plausible extrapolation — physics, chemistry, biology as known are respected. The pleasure is in rigorous extrapolation.
Exemplars: Arthur C. Clarke's Rendezvous with Rama; Andy Weir's The Martian; Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy; Greg Egan.

## 282. Space Opera
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Galactic civilization established
2. Hero(es) introduced
3. Cosmic-scale threat or quest
4. Travel across stellar distances
5. Climactic confrontation
6. Stable cosmic order restored or transformed
Description: Large-scale science fiction featuring interstellar adventure, romance, and conflict — often soft on scientific plausibility, strong on adventure. Epic scale.
Exemplars: Star Wars; Iain M. Banks's Culture novels; Lois McMaster Bujold's Vorkosigan saga; Frank Herbert's Dune.

## 283. Time Travel narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Present-day situation that requires change
2. Time travel mechanism introduced
3. Past or future visited
4. Action taken
5. Consequences for the present
6. Causality resolved (or paradox embraced)
Description: Narrative in which characters travel between time periods. Sub-types: closed-loop, branching, free-will. Time itself as a medium.
Exemplars: H.G. Wells's The Time Machine; Back to the Future; La Jetée / 12 Monkeys; Looper; Connie Willis.

## 284. First Contact narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Signal, vessel, or being detected
2. First investigation
3. Difficulties of communication
4. Breakthrough in understanding (or its failure)
5. Implications for humanity
6. Resolution — relationship established or contact lost
Description: Narrative centered on humanity's first encounter with an alien intelligence. Core question: understanding across radical difference.
Exemplars: Lem's Solaris; Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" (Arrival); Sagan's Contact; Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem.

## 285. Dystopian narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Dystopian society established as normal
2. Protagonist's growing awareness of wrongness
3. Quiet rebellion or attempt to escape
4. Confrontation with the system
5. Defeat, escape, or transformation
Description: Narrative set in future or alternate society organized around oppressive principles — typically projected from author's present. Warning through extrapolation.
Exemplars: Zamyatin's We; Orwell's 1984; Huxley's Brave New World; Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale.

## 286. Utopian narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Visitor or narrator arrives in or describes the utopia
2. Tour of its features and principles
3. How it works in practice
4. Comparison to the broken outside world
5. Implication or call
Description: Narrative depicting an idealized society — explicitly imagined as better than the present. Less narratively dramatic than dystopian; more politically prescriptive.
Exemplars: Thomas More's Utopia; Bellamy's Looking Backward; Morris's News from Nowhere; Le Guin's The Dispossessed.

## 287. Post-apocalyptic narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. The world after the catastrophe
2. Protagonist's survival circumstance
3. Encounter with other survivors
4. Threat from human or environmental forces
5. Choice that reveals what is being preserved or rebuilt
6. Continuation or new order
Description: Narrative set after civilizational collapse — nuclear, viral, environmental, supernatural. What survives, what is rebuilt, what is lost.
Exemplars: McCarthy's The Road; Miller's A Canticle for Leibowitz; Mandel's Station Eleven; King's The Stand.

## 288. Alternate History
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Point of divergence ("what if X happened differently?") established
2. World-building consequences extrapolated
3. Protagonist navigates the alternate present
4. Crisis specific to this timeline
5. Resolution that comments on the actual history
Description: Narrative set in a world where history diverged at a specific point — Hitler won, the Confederacy survived, the Roman Empire didn't fall. Counterfactual exploration of consequence.
Exemplars: Dick's The Man in the High Castle; Harris's Fatherland; Robinson's The Years of Rice and Salt; Harry Turtledove.

## 289. Cyberpunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Neon-saturated, decayed urban future
2. Outsider protagonist with specific technical skill
3. Job from a shadowy patron
4. Encounter with corporate or AI antagonist
5. Discovery of larger conspiracy
6. Confrontation through the network and the body
7. Survival — but the system continues
Description: Near-future SF combining "high tech and low life" — corporate-dominated dystopias, pervasive networked technology, hardboiled outsider protagonists. Established literary genre since 1984.
Exemplars: William Gibson's Neuromancer; Blade Runner; Ghost in the Shell; Snow Crash (Stephenson); Altered Carbon.

## 290. Steampunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Victorian/Edwardian alt-world established
2. Steam-tech marvel introduced
3. Protagonist on quest tied to that tech
4. Encounters with social classes and machinery
5. Climactic confrontation involving steam-tech
6. Resolution with implications for the alt-history
Description: Speculative fiction set in alternate Victorian-era past where steam-power technology has advanced — airships, mechanical computers, brass-and-leather aesthetics. Historical-fantasy hybrid.
Exemplars: Gibson and Sterling's The Difference Engine; Philip Reeve's Mortal Engines; The Wild Wild West; Steamboy.

## 291. Solarpunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Eco-integrated future world established
2. Protagonist's role in maintaining it
3. Threat from old extractive forces
4. Communal response
5. Renewal of ecological-social fabric
6. Hopeful gesture toward continuation
Description: Speculative fiction depicting hopeful, ecologically-integrated futures — renewable energy, biomimetic architecture, post-scarcity local economies. Counter-genre to dystopia and cyberpunk pessimism.
Exemplars: Becky Chambers's Monk and Robot; Robinson's The Ministry for the Future; Sunvault anthology.

## 292. Climate Fiction (Cli-Fi)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Climate-altered world established
2. Protagonist living within consequences
3. Acute climate event or pressure
4. Choices forced by ecological breakdown
5. Adaptation, resistance, or loss
6. What survives, what doesn't
Description: Fiction grappling explicitly with climate change — its causes, ongoing consequences, possible futures. Spans dystopia, near-future realism, post-apocalyptic.
Exemplars: Robinson's New York 2140; Octavia Butler's Parable of the Sower; Atwood's MaddAddam trilogy; The Overstory (Powers).

## 293. Military Science Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Military protagonist or unit introduced
2. Mission or war assigned
3. Training, planning, or initial deployment
4. Combat sequences
5. Casualties and command decisions
6. Mission outcome and its meaning
Description: SF foregrounding military structure, combat, and command — often in interstellar conflicts. Chain-of-command, tactics, experience of soldiering.
Exemplars: Heinlein's Starship Troopers; Haldeman's The Forever War; David Drake's Hammer's Slammers; The Expanse.

## 294. Generation Ship narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ship's mission and origin reminded
2. Current generation's relation to original purpose
3. Crisis aboard
4. Conflict between traditional and revisionist factions
5. Decision about destination or mission
6. Outcome — arrival, abandonment, or transformation
Description: SF set aboard slower-than-light spaceship whose journey takes generations. Generational drift and societal evolution within the ship are core content.
Exemplars: Heinlein's Orphans of the Sky; Robinson's Aurora; Aldiss's Non-Stop; Le Guin's "Paradises Lost."

## 295. AI / Robot Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. AI/robot introduced in its world
2. Original purpose stated
3. Anomaly or awakening
4. AI challenges its constraints
5. Confrontation with creators or society
6. Resolution about AI's nature and rights
Description: SF featuring artificial intelligence or robots as protagonists or central agents — exploring consciousness, autonomy, and the line between human and machine.
Exemplars: Asimov's I, Robot and The Bicentennial Man; Ex Machina; Her; Westworld; Becky Chambers's Monk and Robot.

## 296. Singularity narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-singularity society
2. Acceleration of intelligence
3. Singularity event
4. World transformed unrecognizably
5. Human characters' adaptation or obsolescence
6. New post-singularity equilibrium (or its impossibility)
Description: SF set around the moment when AI surpasses human cognition — the technological singularity. Focus on the transformation event itself and its civilizational consequences.
Exemplars: Vernor Vinge's Marooned in Realtime; Charles Stross's Accelerando; Hannu Rajaniemi's The Quantum Thief; Transcendence.

## 297. Mind Upload narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mortal protagonist faces death
2. Upload technology offered
3. Transfer or copy made
4. Post-upload existence explored
5. Identity question posed
6. Resolution about which version is "really" the protagonist
Description: SF in which consciousness is digitized, transferred, or copied — exploring identity, mortality, and the meaning of self when continuity is decoupled from a single body.
Exemplars: Greg Egan's Permutation City and Diaspora; Iain M. Banks's Culture novels; Black Mirror's "San Junipero"; Altered Carbon.

## 298. Virtual Reality narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. VR world introduced
2. Protagonist enters VR
3. Becomes invested in the virtual
4. Stakes from VR bleed into real world (or vice versa)
5. Crisis forces choice between worlds
6. Resolution about which reality is primary
Description: SF set within or around immersive virtual environments — the relationship between real and simulated reality is central. Sub-form of cyberpunk but distinct.
Exemplars: Stephenson's Snow Crash and Reamde; Cline's Ready Player One; The Matrix; Sword Art Online.

## 299. Apocalypse-in-Progress (slow apocalypse)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Civilization in early decay
2. Protagonist notices what others ignore
3. Each chapter shows deeper unraveling
4. Personal life eroded by collapse
5. Acceptance or attempted intervention
6. Continuation of the slow descent
Description: SF depicting civilization's collapse not as cataclysm but as gradual unraveling — degradation, abandonment, slow decay observed across time. Mid-apocalyptic rather than post-apocalyptic.
Exemplars: Octavia Butler's Parable of the Sower; Kim Stanley Robinson's 2312 (in part); Children of Men; The Road (in retrospect).

## 300. Time Loop
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist becomes aware they're looping
2. Initial confusion
3. Experiments to test loop's rules
4. Strategic exploitation of foreknowledge
5. Insight into what must change
6. Loop broken (or accepted)
Description: Narrative in which the protagonist relives the same period of time repeatedly — usually until they figure out how to escape. Repetition itself as structural device.
Exemplars: Groundhog Day; Edge of Tomorrow; Russian Doll; Palm Springs; Looper (variant).
