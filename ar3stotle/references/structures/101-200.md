# Structures 101-200

## 101. Temptation (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist established with their values
2. Temptation introduced
3. Resistance gives way; protagonist yields
4. Consequences fall — internal and external
5. Reckoning — repentance, ruin, or rationalization
Description: Protagonist faces moral test in the form of attractive but forbidden choice. Internal struggle is the center.
Exemplars: Faust; The Picture of Dorian Gray; Wall Street; The Talented Mr. Ripley.

## 102. Metamorphosis (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Original state established
2. Transformation event — sudden or insidious
3. Adjustment to the new form
4. Crisis brought on by the transformation
5. Resolution — restored, accepted, or destroyed
Description: Character undergoes literal physical or supernatural transformation. Distinct from internal Transformation: this is external/visible.
Exemplars: Kafka's The Metamorphosis; The Fly; Beauty and the Beast (Beast's perspective); The Shape of Water.

## 103. Transformation (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Initial self established
2. Catalyst forces change
3. Resistance and struggle
4. Acceptance and integration
5. New self stabilized
Description: Internal transformation arc. Character is fundamentally changed by experience. Payoff is who the protagonist becomes.
Exemplars: A Christmas Carol; Groundhog Day; Eat, Pray, Love; The King's Speech.

## 104. Maturation (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Young protagonist in protected or limited world
2. Inciting event ruptures the protection
3. Confrontation with adult complications and disillusionment
4. Decision that proves maturity
5. Settling into adult identity
Description: Coming-of-age plot. Young protagonist confronts adult world and matures through hardship. The bildungsroman shape.
Exemplars: The Catcher in the Rye; To Kill a Mockingbird; Stand by Me; Boyhood.

## 105. Love (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lovers meet
2. Initial attraction — obstacles emerge
3. Separation or threatened separation
4. Crisis — love proven against obstacle
5. Union (or fated separation)
Description: Two people meet, kept apart by obstacle, come together. Classical love-plot in simplest form. Desire crossing obstacle.
Exemplars: Pride and Prejudice; When Harry Met Sally; Romeo and Juliet; Notting Hill.

## 106. Forbidden Love (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lovers from incompatible worlds meet
2. Love forms in secret
3. Society or family discovers/forbids
4. Lovers must choose: defy or conform
5. Tragic loss or hard-won union
Description: Love is forbidden by society, family, or moral law — and that prohibition IS the plot. Love against social structure.
Exemplars: Romeo and Juliet; Tristan and Iseult; Brokeback Mountain; The English Patient.

## 107. Sacrifice (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist values something deeply
2. Threat or moral demand emerges
3. Choice between self and greater good crystallizes
4. Sacrifice given
5. Greater good preserved; protagonist's loss honored
Description: Protagonist must give up something dear to achieve a greater good. Climax is the sacrifice itself.
Exemplars: A Tale of Two Cities; Casablanca; Saving Private Ryan; Schindler's List.

## 108. Discovery (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Comfortable unawareness established
2. Disturbing clue or hint emerges
3. Investigation begins, often reluctantly
4. Hidden truth surfaces
5. Reorganization of self around the new knowledge
Description: Character uncovers truth about themselves — identity, origins, or past. Investigation is biographical-internal.
Exemplars: Oedipus Rex; Star Wars: The Empire Strikes Back; Atonement; The Sixth Sense.

## 109. Wretched Excess (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Initial appetite shown as appealing
2. Gratification escalates
3. Costs accumulate, ignored
4. Moment of clarity — refused or accepted too late
5. Destruction by the appetite
Description: Protagonist destroyed by their own appetites — gluttony, lust, ambition, addiction. Excess of a single drive consuming its host.
Exemplars: Requiem for a Dream; Leaving Las Vegas; Citizen Kane; The Wolf of Wall Street.

## 110. Ascension (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Character in modest position with potential
2. First step up — initial recognition
3. Continued ascent against obstacles
4. Defining act of greatness
5. Stature achieved and acknowledged
Description: Character rises to greatness — moral, social, or accomplishment-based. Upward-arc; climax is achievement of stature.
Exemplars: Lincoln; Joan of Arc; Gandhi (Attenborough); Lawrence of Arabia.

## 111. Descension (Tobias)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Character at heights — power, prestige, security
2. First crack — small loss or mistake
3. Pattern of decline accelerates
4. Final disgrace or collapse
5. Bottom — sometimes with possibility of rebirth
Description: Character falls from great position. Fall-arc; gradual loss of stature, position, or self.
Exemplars: King Lear; Sunset Boulevard; There Will Be Blood; The Godfather Part II.

## 112. Polti's 36 Dramatic Situations (taxonomic)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Supplication, 2. Deliverance, 3. Crime pursued by vengeance, 4. Vengeance for kin, 5. Pursuit, 6. Disaster, 7. Falling prey to cruelty, 8. Revolt, 9. Daring enterprise, 10. Abduction, 11. The enigma, 12. Obtaining, 13. Enmity of kinsmen, 14. Rivalry of kinsmen, 15. Murderous adultery, 16. Madness, 17. Fatal imprudence, 18. Involuntary crimes of love, 19. Slaying of unrecognized kinsman, 20. Self-sacrifice for ideal, 21. Self-sacrifice for kindred, 22. All sacrificed for passion, 23. Necessity of sacrificing loved ones, 24. Rivalry superior/inferior, 25. Adultery, 26. Crimes of love, 27. Discovery of dishonor of loved one, 28. Obstacles to love, 29. An enemy loved, 30. Ambition, 31. Conflict with a god, 32. Mistaken jealousy, 33. Erroneous judgment, 34. Remorse, 35. Recovery of lost one, 36. Loss of loved ones
Description: Polti's exhaustive catalog of every dramatic situation. NOT a sequential beat structure but a taxonomy of situational primitives that deploy within other structural genres. Use as scene-design vocabulary.
Exemplars: Polti's foundational 1895 work uses Greek, French, Goethe drama for each.

## 113. Campbell's Monomyth / Hero's Journey
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Call to Adventure
2. Refusal of the Call
3. Supernatural Aid
4. Crossing the First Threshold
5. Belly of the Whale
6. The Road of Trials
7. Meeting with the Goddess
8. Woman as Temptress
9. Atonement with the Father
10. Apotheosis
11. The Ultimate Boon
12. Refusal of the Return
13. The Magic Flight
14. Rescue from Without
15. Crossing the Return Threshold
16. Master of Two Worlds
17. Freedom to Live
Description: Joseph Campbell's seventeen-stage cyclical structure from cross-cultural mythological analysis. Departure, Initiation, Return.
Exemplars: The Odyssey; Star Wars; The Matrix; The Lion King; Harry Potter.

## 114. Vogler's Writer's Journey (12-stage)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ordinary World
2. Call to Adventure
3. Refusal of the Call
4. Meeting the Mentor
5. Crossing the Threshold
6. Tests, Allies, Enemies
7. Approach to the Inmost Cave
8. The Ordeal
9. Reward (Seizing the Sword)
10. The Road Back
11. Resurrection
12. Return with the Elixir
Description: Christopher Vogler's screenwriting-friendly distillation of Campbell's Monomyth into 12 stages, designed for Hollywood film structure.
Exemplars: Star Wars: A New Hope; The Matrix; Finding Nemo; The Lion King.

## 115. Harmon's Story Circle (8-stage)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. A character is in a zone of comfort
2. But they want something
3. They enter an unfamiliar situation
4. Adapt to it
5. Get what they wanted
6. Pay a heavy price for it
7. Return to their familiar situation
8. Having changed
Description: Dan Harmon's compressed 8-step Hero's Journey, used in TV writing (Community, Rick and Morty). Emphasizes return-to-changed-self.
Exemplars: Most Rick and Morty episodes; Community ("Remedial Chaos Theory"); many The Simpsons episodes.

## 116. Save the Cat (15-beat)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening Image
2. Theme Stated
3. Set-Up
4. Catalyst
5. Debate
6. Break into Two
7. B Story
8. Fun and Games
9. Midpoint
10. Bad Guys Close In
11. All Is Lost
12. Dark Night of the Soul
13. Break into Three
14. Finale
15. Final Image
Description: Blake Snyder's 15-beat screenplay structure, with each beat assigned to a specific page range in a 110-page screenplay. Highly prescriptive Hollywood feature standard.
Exemplars: Miss Congeniality; Legally Blonde; most modern Hollywood mid-budget films.

## 117. Truby's 22-Step Anatomy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Self-revelation, need, and desire (foreshadowed)
2. Ghost and story world
3. Weakness and need
4. Inciting event
5. Desire
6. Ally or allies
7. Opponent and/or mystery
8. Fake-ally opponent
9. First revelation and decision: changed desire and motive
10. Plan
11. Opponent's plan and main counterattack
12. Drive
13. Attack by ally
14. Apparent defeat
15. Second revelation and decision
16. Audience revelation
17. Third revelation and decision
18. Gate, gauntlet, visit to death
19. Battle
20. Self-revelation
21. Moral decision
22. New equilibrium
Description: John Truby's 22-step structure: built around moral premise, ghost (past wound), desire, opponent, plan, battle, self-revelation. Story as organic moral argument.
Exemplars: The Godfather; Casablanca; Tootsie; L.A. Confidential.

## 118. Propp's 31 Folktale Functions
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Absentation, 2. Interdiction, 3. Violation, 4. Reconnaissance, 5. Delivery, 6. Trickery, 7. Complicity, 8. Villainy or Lack, 9. Mediation, 10. Beginning Counteraction, 11. Departure, 12. First Function of the Donor, 13. Hero's Reaction, 14. Receipt of a Magical Agent, 15. Guidance, 16. Struggle, 17. Branding, 18. Victory, 19. Liquidation, 20. Return, 21. Pursuit, 22. Rescue, 23. Unrecognized Arrival, 24. Unfounded Claims, 25. Difficult Task, 26. Solution, 27. Recognition, 28. Exposure, 29. Transfiguration, 30. Punishment, 31. Wedding
Description: Vladimir Propp's structural analysis of 100 Russian folk tales yielded 31 narrative functions in fixed order. Foundational to structuralist narratology.
Exemplars: Russian folktales; The Frog Prince; Cinderella (in Propp's analysis); European folktale corpus.

## 119. In medias res / Homeric epic structure
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening in medias res — reader dropped into a charged scene
2. Crisis intensifies briefly
3. Frame device or character begins flashback
4. Backstory unfolds, building toward the opening scene
5. Catch-up to the opening; narrative continues forward
6. Climax and resolution
Description: Classical epic structural convention: narrative begins in middle of action (typically near climax), uses flashback or framed retelling for earlier events.
Exemplars: The Iliad; The Odyssey; Paradise Lost; The Sound and the Fury; Pulp Fiction.

## 120. Murdock's Heroine's Journey
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Separation from the feminine
2. Identification with the masculine
3. Road of trials
4. Illusory boon of success
5. Awakening to feelings of spiritual aridity
6. Initiation and descent to the goddess
7. Urgent yearning to reconnect with the feminine
8. Healing the mother/daughter split
9. Healing the wounded masculine within
10. Integration of masculine and feminine
Description: Maureen Murdock's 10-stage cyclical journey: separation from feminine, identification with masculine, eventual disillusionment, reintegration with wounded feminine self.
Exemplars: Eat Pray Love; Wild (Strayed); Frozen (Anna's arc); The Devil Wears Prada.

## 121. Hudson's Virgin's Promise
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Dependent world
2. Price of conformity
3. Opportunity to shine
4. Dresses the part
5. Secret world
6. No longer fits her world
7. Caught shining
8. Gives up what kept her stuck
9. Kingdom in chaos
10. Wanders in the wilderness
11. Chooses her light
12. Re-ordering (rescue)
13. The kingdom is brighter
Description: Kim Hudson's 13-stage feminine creative-emergence journey. Protagonist begins in suppressive community, secretly cultivates her gift, is exposed, ultimately transforms her community.
Exemplars: Brave (Pixar); Mulan; Moana; Billy Elliot; Whale Rider.

## 122. Vonnegut's Six Story Shapes (taxonomic)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Man in a Hole — falls into trouble, climbs out
2. Boy Meets Girl — finds, loses, regains something wonderful
3. From Bad to Worse — steady descent
4. Which Way is Up — ambiguous oscillation
5. Creation Story — steady rise from nothing
6. Cinderella — small rise, sudden fall, sudden greater rise
Description: Kurt Vonnegut's emotional-arc taxonomy. Stories plotted on good fortune/ill fortune over time yield one of six recurring shapes.
Exemplars: Vonnegut illustrated each with classics — Cinderella, The Metamorphosis (Bad-to-Worse), biblical narratives (Creation).

## 123. Expository documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Authoritative voice opens with thesis
2. Visual evidence supports each claim
3. Counterevidence acknowledged and addressed
4. Conclusion synthesizes argument
Description: Voice-of-God narration directly addresses viewer to convey information, building an argument supported by visual evidence. Most traditional documentary mode.
Exemplars: The Civil War (Burns); Cosmos (Sagan); Attenborough nature documentaries.

## 124. Observational documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Subject's life or event begins without framing
2. Camera observes; no narration
3. Significant moments unfold without direction
4. Camera leaves; closure is what was witnessed
Description: Fly-on-the-wall mode. Camera observes events without narration, interviews, or apparent intervention. Viewer positioned as witness.
Exemplars: Frederick Wiseman (Titicut Follies, High School); Maysles brothers (Salesman, Grey Gardens); Hoop Dreams.

## 125. Participatory documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Filmmaker's presence and project established
2. Encounter with subjects
3. Filmmaker's questioning shapes the unfolding
4. Reflection on what the encounter has revealed
Description: Filmmaker is visibly present, interacting with subjects. The documentary's truth comes from the encounter between filmmaker and subject.
Exemplars: Michael Moore's films; Werner Herzog's documentaries; Nick Broomfield.

## 126. Reflexive documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Conventional documentary mode established
2. Construction of the documentary made visible
3. Viewer's expectations of "truth" examined
4. New understanding of representation emerges
Description: Documentary that draws attention to its own constructedness — to the conventions, the politics of representation, the impossibility of pure objectivity.
Exemplars: Man with a Movie Camera (Vertov); Sans Soleil (Marker); F for Fake (Welles); The Act of Killing.

## 127. Performative documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Filmmaker's subjective stake established
2. Personal experience drives the inquiry
3. Emotional and embodied moments accumulate
4. Closing personal reflection or address
Description: Foregrounds filmmaker's subjective experience and emotional truth — personal essay, autobiography, embodied perspective. Rejects objectivity in favor of "what this means to me."
Exemplars: Marlon Riggs's Tongues Untied; Sarah Polley's Stories We Tell; Agnès Varda's The Beaches of Agnès.

## 128. Poetic documentary
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Visual/aural motif establishes mood
2. Associative editing builds resonance
3. Pattern intensifies or transforms
4. Closing image holds the residue
Description: Privileges mood, rhythm, visual association over linear argument. Often non-narrative, organized by visual/aural patterns.
Exemplars: Ruttmann's Berlin: Symphony of a Great City; Reggio's Koyaanisqatsi; Fricke's Baraka, Samsara.

## 129. Monroe's Motivated Sequence
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Attention — hook the audience
2. Need — establish a problem or unmet need
3. Satisfaction — present your solution
4. Visualization — paint the future with/without the solution
5. Action — specific call to action
Description: Alan Monroe's 5-step persuasive speech structure (1935). Specifically engineered for behavior change — not just informing but motivating action.
Exemplars: Most TED Talks; nonprofit fundraising appeals; Steve Jobs's iPhone keynote.

## 130. Toulmin Argument Model
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Claim — the position being argued
2. Grounds — evidence supporting the claim
3. Warrant — the principle that connects grounds to claim
4. Backing — support for the warrant itself
5. Qualifier — extent or probability
6. Rebuttal — circumstances under which the claim does not hold
Description: Stephen Toulmin's 6-component model of practical argument. Distinguishes from formal logic by acknowledging probability and qualification.
Exemplars: Most academic argumentation; legal briefs; well-constructed op-eds; policy white papers.

## 131. Rogerian Argument
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. State the opposing view fairly and accurately
2. Acknowledge contexts in which the opposing view is valid
3. Present your own view
4. Show contexts in which your view is valid
5. Propose a synthesis or shared higher principle
Description: Carl Rogers-influenced argumentative form built around establishing common ground before advancing one's position. Use when audience holds opposing views and adversarial rhetoric would harden positions.
Exemplars: Conflict-resolution writing; The Atlantic's "I Used to Believe" series; mediation literature.

## 132. Socratic Dialogue
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Initial confident claim by interlocutor
2. Question that exposes a premise
3. Interlocutor's confusion or contradiction
4. Revised claim
5. Further questioning narrows the truth
6. Recognition of what the interlocutor did not know
Description: Plato's didactic dialogue form. Teacher leads interlocutor through questions that expose confused premises. Teaching is in the questioning, not the assertion.
Exemplars: Plato's dialogues (Meno, Republic, Euthyphro); Socratic-method legal education; Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach.

## 133. Aristotelian Triple Appeal (Ethos/Pathos/Logos)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ethos — speaker's credibility, character, authority
2. Pathos — audience's emotional engagement
3. Logos — argument's logical structure and evidence
Description: Aristotle's three modes of persuasion. Not a sequential beat structure but a triad of registers any persuasive text combines.
Exemplars: Lincoln's Gettysburg Address; MLK's "I Have a Dream"; great political speeches generally.

## 134. Inverted Pyramid (journalism)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lead — who, what, when, where, why, how
2. Important supporting details
3. Quotes and context
4. Background and tangential information
Description: Defining structure of news writing — most important information first, supporting details next, background last. Designed for telegraph-era news.
Exemplars: Wire-service news (AP, Reuters); breaking news in major newspapers; NYT front-page hard news.

## 135. Nut-graf feature lead
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Anecdotal or scene-setting lead
2. Nut graf — "this story is about [larger phenomenon]"
3. Body — supporting reporting, examples, perspectives
4. Closing kicker
Description: Feature-style structure: anecdotal opening followed by paragraph explaining why the story matters. The reader is invited in by scene before being told the stakes.
Exemplars: WSJ front-page features (canonical); New Yorker reportage; long-form magazine journalism.

## 136. Tick-Tock
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setup — the situation just before
2. Hour-by-hour or moment-by-moment reconstruction
3. Each scene moves time forward
4. Climactic event reached at its actual chronological point
5. Brief immediate aftermath
Description: Reconstructed-chronology narrative. A major event retold beat-by-beat in real time, often using interviews and documents.
Exemplars: WaPo tick-tocks of 9/11, Capitol riot, election nights; Politico post-election reconstructions.

## 137. Profile / Portrait
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening scene — subject in characteristic action
2. Initial telling detail
3. Backstory and rise
4. Current situation; what is at stake now
5. Defining encounter or moment with the writer
6. Closing image that crystallizes the subject
Description: Journalistic biographical narrative of a single person. Present scene → backstory → present significance. Tied to a present news peg.
Exemplars: Talese's "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold"; New Yorker profiles; Esquire profiles; Vanity Fair features.

## 138. Long-form literary journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Scene that opens with vivid specificity
2. Cast of characters established
3. Reportorial backstory and context interwoven
4. Multiple scenes build the narrative arc
5. Climactic event or revelation
6. Closing scene that returns or transforms the opening
Description: Extended reported nonfiction using techniques of fiction — scene-by-scene, full dialogue, interior perspective, status detail — to render real events.
Exemplars: Capote's In Cold Blood; Didion's Slouching Towards Bethlehem; Wolfe's The Right Stuff; Orlean's The Orchid Thief.

## 139. Investigative narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Anomaly or tip surfaces
2. Initial reporting raises questions
3. Resistance from subjects encountered
4. Documents and sources break the story open
5. Pattern of wrongdoing established
6. Confrontation and accountability sought
7. Story published; consequences follow
Description: Reported narrative whose engine is the journalist's investigation itself — gradual uncovering of hidden facts, often involving institutional wrongdoing.
Exemplars: All the President's Men (Woodward-Bernstein); Spotlight (Boston Globe); Farrow's Catch and Kill; ProPublica.

## 140. Case Study
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Company and situation introduced
2. Decision point — what choice was faced
3. Options considered
4. Path taken
5. Outcome
6. Analysis — what made it work or fail
7. Lessons applicable beyond this case
Description: Structured narrative of specific business situation, decision, and outcome. Used in education, consulting, B2B marketing. Analytic register; narrative serves transferable lessons.
Exemplars: Harvard Business School cases; McKinsey case studies; HubSpot customer cases; HBR case features.

## 141. Pitch Deck Story
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Problem in the world
2. Why now (market timing)
3. Solution
4. How it works (product)
5. Market size and opportunity
6. Traction or proof
7. Team
8. Ask
Description: Narrative arc of a startup investor pitch. Engineered for 10-15 slides and 15 minutes. Establishes need and offer in tight time.
Exemplars: YC pitch deck template; Sequoia template; Airbnb's 2008 pitch deck.

## 142. Brand Origin Story
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Founder's pre-company life
2. Specific moment of insight or unmet need
3. Decision to pursue
4. Early struggle or near-failure
5. Breakthrough that validated the path
6. Present-day significance
Description: Company's foundational narrative — founder's moment of insight, unmet need perceived, early struggle, present significance. Creates emotional attachment to brand.
Exemplars: Nike (Phil Knight's car); Apple's garage; Patagonia's Yvon Chouinard; Airbnb's air mattresses.

## 143. Customer Success Story
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Customer's pre-product situation (the pain)
2. Discovery of the product
3. Implementation and early skepticism
4. Specific moment of value realization
5. Quantified outcome and present state
Description: Narrative of specific customer's transformation through using a product. Before-state → catalyst → after-state, with measurable outcomes.
Exemplars: Salesforce customer cases; nonprofit impact reports; HBR customer features.

## 144. Crisis Communication
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledge the situation immediately and specifically
2. Express empathy for those affected
3. Take responsibility (where appropriate)
4. Explain what is being done now
5. Explain what will prevent recurrence
6. Commit to ongoing transparency
Description: Structured corporate response to scandal, accident, or public failure. Acknowledge harm, accept responsibility, articulate corrective action, restore trust.
Exemplars: J&J Tylenol response (1982 gold standard); Domino's 2009 video response; Boeing 737 MAX (negative example).

## 145. Disruption Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Incumbent dominance established
2. Disruptor enters with seemingly inferior product
3. Incumbent dismisses the threat
4. Disruptor improves rapidly along an unexpected dimension
5. Market redefined; incumbent wrong-footed
6. Present-day market reorganized around disruptor
Description: Christensen's framing as public narrative: incumbent industry leader overtaken by smaller competitor offering "worse" product that redefines market.
Exemplars: Netflix vs Blockbuster; Tesla vs legacy auto; Uber vs taxis; Airbnb vs hotels; Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma.

## 146. White Paper
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Executive summary
2. Problem definition
3. Current state of solutions and their limitations
4. Proposed approach
5. Detailed analysis and evidence
6. Implementation considerations
7. Conclusion and call to action
Description: Long-form authoritative document defining a problem, examining existing solutions, advocating a specific approach. Used in B2B marketing, policy advocacy.
Exemplars: Government policy white papers; Bitcoin whitepaper (Satoshi 2008); enterprise software vendor whitepapers.

## 147. IMRAD Research Paper
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Introduction — problem, prior work, hypothesis
2. Methods — how the research was conducted
3. Results — what was found
4. Discussion — what the results mean, limitations, implications
Description: Standard structure of empirical scientific papers. Designed for reproducibility and rapid expert reading.
Exemplars: Most empirical scientific papers since mid-20th c.; Nature, Science, biomedical journals.

## 148. Literature Review
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Topic and scope defined
2. Search and inclusion criteria stated
3. Major theoretical frameworks summarized
4. Empirical findings organized thematically
5. Contested questions and tensions identified
6. Gaps in current literature
7. Implications for future research
Description: Academic synthesis of existing research — a structured map of the field. Identifies major findings, contested questions, methodological approaches, gaps.
Exemplars: Annual Review articles; doctoral dissertation literature-review chapters; systematic reviews in medicine.

## 149. Position Paper / Op-Ed
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hook — a vivid case or news peg
2. Position stated clearly
3. Stakes — why it matters now
4. Supporting argument with evidence
5. Anticipation of counterarguments
6. Call to action or implication
Description: Short-to-medium argumentative essay advocating a specific position. Compressed for journalistic or institutional reading.
Exemplars: NYT op-eds; Atlantic essays; institutional position papers; academic policy essays.

## 150. Grant Proposal
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Project summary / abstract
2. Statement of need / problem
3. Goals and objectives
4. Methodology / approach
5. Evaluation plan
6. Team qualifications
7. Budget and budget justification
8. Sustainability plan
Description: Structured request for funding research, programs, or projects. Distinguished by dual audience: program officers and reviewers.
Exemplars: NIH R01 grants; NSF proposals; foundation grants (Ford, MacArthur, Gates); humanities NEH grants.

## 151. Opening Statement (legal)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Theme of the case stated in one sentence
2. Brief story of what happened
3. Identification of key witnesses and what they will say
4. Promise of what the evidence will show
5. What the lawyer will ask the jury to find
Description: First address by lawyer to jury, previewing the case. Strictly bounded — must be roadmap, not argument; promises evidence rather than asserting conclusions.
Exemplars: Iconic opening statements: O.J. Simpson trial; Atticus Finch in To Kill a Mockingbird; A Few Good Men.

## 152. Closing Argument (legal)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Restatement of the theme
2. Story of what happened, now with evidentiary backing
3. Why the evidence proves the claim
4. Anticipation and rebuttal of opposing argument
5. Specific verdict requested
Description: Lawyer's final address to jury, connecting evidence to verdict requested. Argument is permitted; lawyer is now persuading rather than previewing.
Exemplars: Atticus Finch's closing in To Kill a Mockingbird; Clarence Darrow's closings; 12 Angry Men.

## 153. Stump Speech
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Local greeting / acknowledgment
2. Personal story that grounds the candidate
3. Diagnosis of what is wrong
4. Vision of what could be
5. Specific policy commitments
6. Call to action — vote, volunteer, donate
Description: Repeated political speech delivered across many campaign stops. Modular and emotionally consistent — same emotional arc through varying details.
Exemplars: Obama's 2008 campaign stump; Bernie Sanders 2016/2020; classical campaign speeches.

## 154. Inaugural Address
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledgment of the moment and predecessors
2. Naming of the historical challenge or opportunity
3. Invocation of shared national values
4. Vision and call to collective effort
5. Ceremonial close — often invoking divine or historical witness
Description: Ceremonial speech by incoming head of state. Distinguished by move from contested partisanship to symbolic unity.
Exemplars: Lincoln's Second Inaugural; FDR's First Inaugural; JFK's "ask not"; Obama 2009.

## 155. Concession Speech
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledgment of the loss
2. Congratulation of the winner
3. Recognition of the campaign's supporters
4. Affirmation of shared democratic values
5. Forward-looking commitment to civic engagement
Description: Speech of defeated candidate acknowledging loss and legitimacy of opponent's victory. The transfer of legitimacy IS the engine.
Exemplars: Al Gore 2000; John McCain 2008; Hillary Clinton 2016.

## 156. Manifesto
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Diagnosis of the broken present
2. Identification of the responsible parties or systems
3. Declaration of principles
4. Vision of the new order
5. Call to immediate action or alignment
Description: Declarative public statement of intent or principles by a movement. Designed to provoke alignment and rupture. Galvanizes rather than persuades.
Exemplars: Marx and Engels's Communist Manifesto; Futurist Manifesto (Marinetti); Port Huron Statement; Haraway's Cyborg Manifesto.

## 157. Whistleblower Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Insider's position and initial belief in the institution
2. Discovery of wrongdoing
3. Internal reporting attempted and rebuffed
4. Decision to go public
5. Personal cost paid
6. Implications for the institution and the public
Description: First-person account of insider exposing institutional wrongdoing, often at personal cost. Authority comes from inside position.
Exemplars: Snowden's Permanent Record; Ellsberg's Secrets; Frances Haugen; Wigand (basis for The Insider).

## 158. Cosmic Horror (Lovecraftian)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Scholar or investigator encounters disturbing artifact or text
2. Investigation reveals fragments of cosmic truth
3. Each new revelation is worse than the last
4. Glimpse of the actual cosmic horror
5. Investigator's mind cracks under the truth
6. Closing — suggestion that the horror persists
Description: Horror based on smallness of human consciousness against indifferent or malevolent cosmos. The threat is ontological, not personal.
Exemplars: Lovecraft's "Call of Cthulhu," "At the Mountains of Madness"; Blackwood's "The Willows"; True Detective S1; Annihilation (VanderMeer).

## 159. Gothic Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent protagonist arrives at gloomy ancestral place
2. Strange occurrences accumulate
3. Family secret hinted at
4. Confrontation with the buried past
5. Climactic revelation, often with violence
6. House or estate destroyed or transformed
Description: Horror anchored in atmospheric place — haunted house, ruined castle, decaying estate — and family secrets, suppressed sexuality, return of the buried past.
Exemplars: Walpole's Castle of Otranto; Wuthering Heights; Jane Eyre; Rebecca; The Haunting of Hill House; Crimson Peak.

## 160. Whodunit
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Murder discovered in a contained setting
2. Detective arrives; suspects introduced
3. Investigation; clues planted
4. False solutions explored and disproven
5. Detective gathers all suspects
6. Reveal of the true murderer with reasoning
Description: Puzzle-mystery in which detective gathers clues to identify killer from closed cast of suspects. The pleasure is the deduction; clues are fairly available to careful reader.
Exemplars: Christie's And Then There Were None, Murder on the Orient Express; Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes; Knives Out; Glass Onion.

## 161. Hardboiled Detective
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Detective hired for a seemingly small case
2. First-person narrator establishes voice and city
3. Case opens up — wider corruption hinted
4. Beatings, betrayals, ambiguous women
5. Climactic confrontation
6. Resolution that does not restore order — corruption persists
Description: Detective story rooted in urban realism, moral ambiguity, cynical first-person narrator. The puzzle is secondary to atmosphere and detective's character.
Exemplars: Hammett's The Maltese Falcon; Chandler's The Big Sleep; Mosley's Easy Rawlins; Chinatown; Mystic River.

## 162. Cozy Mystery
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cozy setting and community established
2. Murder occurs (often off-page)
3. Amateur detective begins investigating
4. Suspects are community members
5. Detective uses local knowledge to solve
6. Murderer revealed; community restored
Description: Puzzle-mystery in low-violence pastoral or small-town setting with amateur detective. The world is one the reader wants to return to.
Exemplars: Christie's Miss Marple; Murder, She Wrote; Hamish Macbeth; Only Murders in the Building.

## 163. Bildungsroman
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Childhood — protagonist's inherited circumstance
2. Departure from home into wider world
3. Sequence of formative encounters and lessons
4. First serious love
5. Disillusionment or crisis
6. Re-orientation and decision about life path
7. Settled adult identity
Description: European novel of formation. Young protagonist's psychological and moral development from naïve youth through formative trials to mature adulthood.
Exemplars: Goethe's Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship; David Copperfield; Great Expectations; Portrait of the Artist; To Kill a Mockingbird.

## 164. Picaresque
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pícaro introduced as low-born and resourceful
2. Picaresque journey begins — series of episodic encounters
3. Each episode exposes a different social stratum's hypocrisy
4. Pícaro's wits are tested and survive
5. Closing — pícaro continues onward, unchanged
Description: Satirical episodic novel following low-born rogue through adventures across various social strata, exposing each through the rogue's wits. Pícaro does NOT mature — they survive.
Exemplars: Lazarillo de Tormes; Don Quixote (in part); Tom Jones; Huckleberry Finn; Augie March; Catch-22.

## 165. Refusal-of-the-Easy
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Easy and obvious gain offered
2. Quiet refusal that needs no defense
3. Cost of refusal absorbed without complaint
Description: Short narrative whose entire arc is the protagonist's deliberate refusal of an obviously beneficial choice — not because they fail to see its value, but because accepting would betray something they refuse to name. Quiet sovereignty as the resolution.
Exemplars: Cincinnatus narratives; Washington stepping down after two terms; Bartleby's "I would prefer not to" extended into a structural arc.

## 166. The Inversion of Care
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Devoted care established as a relationship's defining trait
2. The care, taken to an extreme or wrong shape, harms its object
3. Recognition that the love and the harm cannot be separated
Description: Narrative in which the act of caring for someone causes the harm it sought to prevent. Captures the loving harmer — good intentions becoming the wound.
Exemplars: Lady Bird (mother's love as criticism); helicopter-parenting narratives; over-protection that accelerates decline.

## 167. Witness-Without-Intervention
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Protagonist arrives in position to witness
2. Sustained, attentive observation of what unfolds
3. Departure — what was seen is now carried by the witness
Description: Narrative in which the protagonist is positioned as witness to something significant — usually injustice or beauty — and the structural payoff is the quality of the witnessing itself, not action taken.
Exemplars: Travel writing; quieter wartime journalism; Didion's California essays; certain photography essays (Salgado).

## 168. Slow-Yes
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Polite, unforced no held
2. Series of small offerings absorbed without acknowledgment
3. Yes arrives quietly, almost without ceremony
Description: Narrative whose entire arc is the gradual softening of a no into a yes — across many small moments rather than a single decisive turn. Cumulative arrival without obstacle.
Exemplars: Late-life-romance narratives; mentor/student arcs; The Remains of the Day (in some readings); Lost in Translation.

## 169. Ledger-Closes
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Old, unsettled obligation established as background
2. Quiet decision and movement to settle it now
3. Settlement made; ledger closed
Description: Narrative whose engine is settling a long-standing moral debt — not revenge (hot, proportional), but cool deliberate return of what was owed, after many years.
Exemplars: Restitution narratives in post-war fiction; The Reader (Schlink); Ishiguro's The Buried Giant; estate-and-inheritance dramas.

## 170. The Held Question
Tradition: Universal (NGC-original)
Beats:
1. Initial question raised, perhaps casually
2. Partial answers and complications deepen the question
3. Question articulated more precisely; resolution declined
Description: Narrative whose payoff is the deepening of a question rather than its resolution. The question itself ripens. Reader leaves more curious, more precisely uncertain.
Exemplars: Borges's "The Library of Babel"; Anne Carson's The Glass Essay; The Crying of Lot 49 (Pynchon); some W.G. Sebald.

## 171. Chosen One narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Ordinary life with hidden marker
2. Prophecy or sign reveals the chosen status
3. Refusal or doubt
4. Acceptance of fate
5. Trials that prove or threaten the chosenness
6. Final confrontation only the chosen can face
Description: Protagonist singled out by prophecy, birth, or supernatural sign as uniquely fated. Distinct from Hero's Journey by its external selection — the call is not refuseable in any meaningful way.
Exemplars: Harry Potter; The Matrix (Neo); Star Wars (Anakin/Luke); Dune (Paul Atreides); Buffy the Vampire Slayer.

## 172. Fish Out of Water
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist established in their native element
2. Sudden relocation to alien environment
3. Failures of adaptation produce comedy or trouble
4. Gradual learning or unexpected competence
5. Resolution — protagonist either adapts or returns transformed
Description: Protagonist placed in environment radically alien to their habits and competencies; the friction between protagonist and setting drives both comedy and growth.
Exemplars: Crocodile Dundee; Coming to America; Lost in Translation; Schitt's Creek.

## 173. Mentor and Pupil
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pupil's need or potential established
2. Mentor encountered, often reluctantly
3. Training or teaching begins
4. Pupil's trial (with or without mentor present)
5. Mentor's lesson tested against reality
6. Pupil surpasses, honors, or buries the mentor
Description: Narrative centered on relationship between experienced master and inexperienced student. The mentor's wisdom is tested as much as the pupil's capacity. Story is about transmission across generations.
Exemplars: Star Wars (Yoda/Luke); Karate Kid; Good Will Hunting; Dead Poets Society; Whiplash.

## 174. Buddy narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Both characters established in their distinct modes
2. Forced pairing
3. Initial friction and incompatibility
4. Mutual adjustment under pressure
5. Trust forged through trial
6. Closing affirmation of the bond
Description: Two protagonists with contrasting temperaments, methods, or backgrounds paired (often forced together). Pleasure is the friction-and-bond between mismatched partners.
Exemplars: Lethal Weapon; Rush Hour; Toy Story; Pulp Fiction (Vincent and Jules); 48 Hrs.

## 175. Ensemble narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cast introduced in their network of relations
2. Inciting situation affects the group
3. Each member's response traced
4. Lines of conflict and alliance form
5. Climactic event involves the whole group
6. New equilibrium for the ensemble
Description: Narrative organized around a group of roughly equal-weight characters whose intersecting arcs constitute the story. The cast IS the story.
Exemplars: The Big Chill; Magnolia; Crash; Love Actually; Friends; The Office.

## 176. Stranger Comes to Town
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Community in stable equilibrium
2. Stranger arrives
3. Stranger's presence disturbs the equilibrium
4. Hidden tensions in the community surface
5. Confrontation with what the stranger represents
6. Community transformed or stranger expelled
Description: One of John Gardner's two foundational stories. Outsider's arrival disrupts static order of a community, exposing tensions and forcing reconfiguration.
Exemplars: Shane; High Noon; The Stranger (Camus); Chocolat; Pale Rider.

## 177. Hero Goes on a Journey
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist embedded in familiar life
2. Decision or compulsion to leave
3. Departure
4. Encounters along the road
5. Furthest point reached
6. Return (or refusal of return)
Description: Gardner's other foundational story. Protagonist leaves the familiar and travels into the unknown. The journey is the form, not necessarily a search.
Exemplars: On the Road; The Wizard of Oz; Wild (Strayed); The Odyssey; many road-movie narratives.

## 178. Sacrificial Lamb
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent figure introduced as community's hope or treasure
2. Threat to community emerges
3. Innocent's vulnerability becomes the crisis
4. Community's survival requires their loss
5. Loss occurs
6. Community reconstituted around the absence
Description: Innocent character must die or suffer to redeem or save a community, often without their full understanding of why. The lamb may not even understand the role.
Exemplars: The Lottery (Jackson); Sophie's Choice; certain biblical narratives; Of Mice and Men (Lennie).

## 179. Riches to Rags
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Wealth, comfort, or position enjoyed
2. First crack — error, betrayal, or external shock
3. Decline accelerates; possessions and status stripped
4. Bottom reached — destitution or ruin
5. Reckoning with the new reality
Description: Mirror of Booker's Rags-to-Riches. Character begins in wealth, status, or comfort and falls — through error, betrayal, or systemic collapse — into deprivation.
Exemplars: King Lear; Joseph Roth's Job; Margin Call (corporate variant); Great Depression narratives; Falling Down.

## 180. Round Robin / Interlocking narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. First story-arc introduced
2. Second story-arc with passing reference to the first
3. Third arc — pattern of connection emerges
4. Climactic convergence or thematic crystallization
5. Closing — pattern revealed
Description: Narrative composed of multiple discrete stories that connect through shared characters, locations, themes, or coincidences. The reader's pleasure is recognition of connections.
Exemplars: Pulp Fiction; Magnolia; Cloud Atlas; A Visit from the Goon Squad (Egan); Olive Kitteridge.

## 181. Narration mode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setting and characters established
2. Events occur in sequence
3. Climactic moment
4. Resolution
Description: Telling a story — sequential events arranged in time. The fundamental rhetorical mode. Use when the goal is to recount what happened.
Exemplars: Most fiction; personal essays; news reports.

## 182. Description mode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Subject named
2. Spatial or sensory orientation
3. Layered detail (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory)
4. Telling specifics that establish character
5. Closing image or impression
Description: Detailed sensory rendering of a person, place, object, or scene. The goal is to make the reader perceive. Use when content is what something is like rather than what happens.
Exemplars: Travel writing; nature writing (Annie Dillard); descriptive passages in literary fiction.

## 183. Exposition mode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Topic introduced
2. Key terms defined
3. Principles explained
4. Examples or applications
5. Synthesis or conclusion
Description: Explanation of an idea, concept, or process. The goal is to make the reader understand. Use when content is informational and the reader needs to grasp how something works.
Exemplars: Most explanatory journalism; textbook prose; Wikipedia articles; popular-science writing.

## 184. Argumentation mode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Claim stated
2. Reasons advanced
3. Evidence presented
4. Counterarguments addressed
5. Conclusion drawn
Description: Reasoning to convince. The mode designed for changing minds. Use when the goal is to advance a contested claim with evidence and reasoning.
Exemplars: Op-eds; legal briefs; academic argumentative essays.

## 185. Comparison and Contrast
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Two or more subjects introduced
2. Common ground established
3. Point-by-point contrast or similarity
4. Synthesis — what the comparison reveals
Description: Structure organized around the systematic juxtaposition of two or more subjects. Use when understanding comes through what is like and what is unlike.
Exemplars: "X vs Y" essays; product comparisons; literary criticism comparing authors.

## 186. Cause and Effect
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Phenomenon to be explained named
2. Background conditions described
3. Causes traced (proximate and ultimate)
4. Effects unfolded
5. Implications drawn
Description: Structure tracing causal chains — what produces what, and why. Use when content is why something happened or what something will produce.
Exemplars: Historical analysis; epidemiological reports; many Atlantic explanatory features.

## 187. Process Analysis
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Goal or end-state of the process named
2. Materials, prerequisites, or starting conditions
3. Sequenced steps
4. Common pitfalls or variations
5. Final state achieved
Description: Step-by-step explanation of how something is done or how something happens. Two sub-modes: directive (instructions) and informative. Use when content is procedural.
Exemplars: Recipes; instructional manuals; Wikihow articles; how-to journalism.

## 188. Definition mode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Term named
2. Genus established (the larger category)
3. Differentia (what distinguishes it from others in the genus)
4. Examples and counter-examples
5. Refinement or boundary discussion
Description: Establishing precisely what a term, concept, or category means — by genus, by negation, by example, by etymology. Use when content's success depends on a term being shared.
Exemplars: Dictionary entries; philosophical essays defining terms; scientific definitions.

## 189. Classification and Division
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Domain to be classified named
2. Principle of division stated
3. Categories enumerated
4. Each category defined and exemplified
5. Boundary cases discussed
Description: Organizing a complex domain into categories (classification) or breaking a single thing into its parts (division). Use when content is taxonomic.
Exemplars: Linnaean biological taxonomy; this very catalog; diagnostic frameworks (DSM-5).

## 190. Syllogism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Major premise
2. Minor premise
3. Conclusion
Description: Aristotle's three-part deductive argument. The foundational form of formal reasoning.
Exemplars: "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal."

## 191. Enthymeme
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Stated premise
2. Stated premise OR conclusion (one missing)
3. Inference completed by audience
Description: Syllogism with one premise unstated — assumed shared with the audience. The most common form of real-world persuasive reasoning.
Exemplars: "He's a politician — of course he's lying"; most editorial argumentation.

## 192. Argument by Analogy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Case A described
2. Case B (the one in question) described
3. Relevant features shared
4. Conclusion: what holds in A likely holds in B
Description: Reasoning that two cases share enough relevant features that what is true of one is likely true of the other. Use when direct evidence is absent but a comparable case can be invoked.
Exemplars: Legal precedent argument; "Inviting them is like inviting a fox into the henhouse"; many policy debates.

## 193. Confirmation-Refutation
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Statement of position
2. Confirmation — positive arguments for your view
3. Refutation — dismantling of opposing view
4. Conclusion
Description: Classical rhetorical structure: first establish your own arguments positively, then dismantle the opposing view. Use when audience is mixed and both sides need addressing.
Exemplars: Cicero's speeches; classical legal argumentation; many traditional sermons.

## 194. Concession-and-Counter
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Acknowledge the opposing point as valid
2. Specify the limits of that validity
3. Pivot to your stronger point
4. Conclude with refined position
Description: A move within argument: explicitly grant a point to the opposing view, then redirect to a stronger point that holds despite the concession. Honest acknowledgment of partial agreement strengthens credibility.
Exemplars: "Yes, the policy has costs — but the costs of inaction are higher"; common in skilled op-eds.

## 195. Reductio ad Absurdum
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opposing premise stated
2. Logical extension carried out
3. Absurd or contradictory consequence reached
4. Premise rejected
Description: Showing that an opponent's premise leads to absurd or contradictory consequences, thereby disproving the premise.
Exemplars: Plato's dialogues; satirical essays (Swift's "A Modest Proposal"); legal argument.

## 196. Bandwagon
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crowd's behavior or belief asserted
2. Implication that joining is natural or inevitable
3. Call to align with the majority
Description: Persuasion arguing "everyone is doing it / believes it / supports it" — therefore you should join. Exploits social proof.
Exemplars: Election advertising; consumer marketing ("Join the millions...").

## 197. Glittering Generality
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Virtuous-sounding term invoked
2. Association with the proposition
3. Audience completes the meaning favorably
Description: Use of vague, virtuous-sounding terms (freedom, justice, family values) without specific content — letting the audience fill in their own meaning.
Exemplars: Most political speech; brand slogans; nationalist rhetoric.

## 198. Card Stacking
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Position to be supported
2. Selective evidence assembled
3. Contradictory evidence omitted
4. Position presented as if comprehensively supported
Description: Selective presentation of evidence — only the facts that support the position, omitting contradictory evidence.
Exemplars: Advertising; tendentious journalism; legal briefs (within ethical bounds).

## 199. Plain Folks appeal
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Speaker's elite or unusual status known
2. Speaker performs ordinary register and concerns
3. Identification fostered
4. Position offered as folk wisdom
Description: Speaker positions themselves as ordinary, just-like-you, to bypass elite-skepticism in the audience.
Exemplars: Political speeches by candidates of privilege; folksy CEO messaging; populist communication.

## 200. Testimonial appeal
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Trusted figure introduced
2. Their endorsement of position presented
3. Transfer of trust to proposition
Description: A respected or admired figure endorses the position or product. Audience trust in a third party transfers to the proposition.
Exemplars: Celebrity ads; expert endorsements; political endorsements.
