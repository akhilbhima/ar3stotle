# Structures 501-600

## 501. Epic Poem
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Invocation of muse
2. Statement of theme
3. In medias res opening
4. Episodic adventures and trials
5. Climactic battle or quest fulfillment
6. Return or founding
Description: Long narrative poem of heroic scale — wars, voyages, founding of nations, divine interventions. Sustained verse register and conventional epic features.
Exemplars: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey; Virgil's Aeneid; Milton's Paradise Lost; Dante's Divine Comedy; Beowulf.

## 502. Mock Epic
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Epic invocation for trivial subject
2. Mock-heroic descriptions
3. Apparent gravity given to small things
4. Parodic battle or confrontation
5. Climactic absurd moment
6. Mock resolution
Description: Epic-form poem applied to trivial subject — using high heroic register to magnify a quarrel over a lock of hair, a frog-mouse battle, etc. Parodic stance toward epic conventions.
Exemplars: Pope's The Rape of the Lock; Pope's The Dunciad; Byron's Don Juan (variant); Boileau's Le Lutrin.

## 503. Heroic Poem (medieval romance)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Knight introduced at court
2. Quest accepted
3. Adventures with monsters and rival knights
4. Encounter with lady
5. Climactic combat or ordeal
6. Return triumphant or transformed
Description: Medieval narrative poem of knightly adventure, courtly love, and chivalric virtue. More individualized hero and romance conventions than classical epic.
Exemplars: Chrétien de Troyes's romances; Sir Gawain and the Green Knight; Ariosto's Orlando Furioso; Spenser's The Faerie Queene.

## 504. Ballad (narrative poem)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Setting and characters in opening stanza
2. Conflict introduced
3. Repeated refrains build tension
4. Climactic event (often death or tragic loss)
5. Aftermath stanza
6. Closing image
Description: Short narrative folk poem, traditionally sung, in regular stanzas. Tells a single dramatic episode — often of love, death, war, or supernatural encounter.
Exemplars: "Sir Patrick Spens"; "Tam Lin"; "Barbara Allen"; Coleridge's Rime of the Ancient Mariner; Bob Dylan's narrative songs.

## 505. Lay
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Brief setup of knight or lady
2. Encounter with supernatural
3. Test or quest
4. Adventure unfolds
5. Climactic moment
6. Closing — often with marvel as memory
Description: Short medieval narrative poem, especially Breton lay — supernatural-tinged adventures, courtly love, fantastic creatures. Compact, literary courtly origin.
Exemplars: Marie de France's Lais; Sir Orfeo; Chaucer's The Franklin's Tale.

## 506. Idyll
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pastoral scene introduced
2. Shepherds or rustic figures
3. Conversation, song-contest, or love
4. Brief drama or contest
5. Resolution within rural calm
6. Closing pastoral image
Description: Short pastoral or idealized poem of rural life — shepherds, lovers, peaceful scenes. Idealized rural register.
Exemplars: Theocritus's Idylls; Tennyson's Idylls of the King (Arthurian variant); Wordsworth's pastoral poems.

## 507. Eclogue
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Two shepherds meet
2. Topic introduced (love, song-contest, lament)
3. Alternating exchange
4. Building toward conclusion
5. Climactic verse
6. Departure or rest
Description: Pastoral dialogue poem — typically two shepherds in conversation. Specific dialogue structure.
Exemplars: Virgil's Eclogues; Spenser's Shepheardes Calender; Pope's Pastorals.

## 508. Pastoral (broader form)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Court or city established as compromised
2. Retreat to rural setting
3. Pastoral encounter (love, leisure, contemplation)
4. Wisdom gained from rural life
5. Return or refusal of return
6. Pastoral truth as resolution
Description: Literary mode idealizing rural life as contrast to corrupt urban or court life. Spans poetry, prose, drama. Country-versus-city register.
Exemplars: Sidney's Arcadia; Shakespeare's As You Like It; Goldsmith's The Deserted Village; Frost's pastoral poems.

## 509. Georgic
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Praise of rural labor
2. Specific agricultural process described
3. Seasonal cycle
4. Hardships and rewards
5. Climactic harvest or moment
6. Reflection on labor's dignity
Description: Poem about agriculture and rural labor — work of farming rather than its idealized leisure. Distinguished from pastoral by its labor focus.
Exemplars: Virgil's Georgics; James Thomson's The Seasons; Wendell Berry's poems (modern variant).

## 510. Elegy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Loss named or invoked
2. Praise of the lost
3. Personal lament
4. Reflection on broader mortality
5. Climactic grief or recognition
6. Resolution: consolation, acceptance, or unresolved sorrow
Description: Poem of mourning — lament for the dead, lost love, or fading time. Meditative, grief-driven register.
Exemplars: Milton's "Lycidas"; Tennyson's In Memoriam; Whitman's "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd"; Auden's "In Memory of W.B. Yeats."

## 511. Ode
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Direct address to subject
2. Praise or invocation
3. Reflection on subject's significance
4. Personal or universal application
5. Climactic stanza of intensity
6. Closing benediction
Description: Lyric poem of elevated address — to a person, thing, or abstract idea. Often public, ceremonial, or contemplative. Formal verse structure and high register.
Exemplars: Pindar's odes; Horace's odes; Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale"; Wordsworth's "Ode: Intimations of Immortality"; Pablo Neruda's odes (modern).

## 512. Sonnet (narrative)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Octave or first quatrains: situation/argument
2. Volta (turn): shift in thought
3. Sestet or final quatrain + couplet: response/resolution
4. Compressed development across 14 lines
Description: 14-line lyric poem with established rhyme scheme (Petrarchan or Shakespearean). Used for love, philosophical reflection, or compressed argument. Volta structure.
Exemplars: Petrarch's Canzoniere; Shakespeare's sonnets; Donne's holy sonnets; Edna St. Vincent Millay; Terrance Hayes's American Sonnets.

## 513. Villanelle
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Refrain 1 introduced
2. Refrain 2 introduced
3. Tercets accumulate, refrains alternating
4. Meaning of refrains evolves through context
5. Climactic stanza brings both refrains together
6. Final quatrain pairs both refrains
Description: 19-line poem with two refrains and only two rhymes — five tercets and a quatrain. The repeating refrains accumulate meaning.
Exemplars: Dylan Thomas's "Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night"; Elizabeth Bishop's "One Art"; Theodore Roethke's "The Waking."

## 514. Sestina
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Six end-words established in first stanza
2. End-words rotate through six stanzas
3. Each stanza recontextualizes the words
4. Climactic stanza brings full pattern
5. Three-line envoi compresses all six end-words
6. Resolution through formal completion
Description: 39-line poem of six six-line stanzas plus a three-line envoi, where six end-words rotate through specific pattern across stanzas. Extreme formal constraint.
Exemplars: Arnaut Daniel's foundational sestinas; Elizabeth Bishop's "Sestina"; Anthony Hecht; Algernon Charles Swinburne.

## 515. Ghazal
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening couplet introduces refrain
2. Each successive couplet a self-contained meditation
3. Refrain accumulates meaning across couplets
4. Beloved invoked (human or divine)
5. Final couplet often signs poet's name
6. Resolution through formal completion
Description: Persian/Arabic/Urdu poetic form: 5-15 couplets, each self-contained, ending with a refrain — often about unrequited or divine love. Couplet-autonomy structure.
Exemplars: Hafiz; Rumi; Mirza Ghalib; Agha Shahid Ali (modern English ghazals).

## 516. Haiku
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Image or scene presented
2. Kireji (cutting word) creates juxtaposition
3. Second image or thought completes the contrast
Description: Japanese 17-mora poem (5-7-5) traditionally including a kigo (season word) and a kireji (cutting word). Extreme compression and seasonal-imagistic register.
Exemplars: Bashō, Buson, Issa; Yosa Buson; modern English haiku poets (Cor van den Heuvel).

## 517. Tanka
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. First three lines (5-7-5) set image or situation
2. Pivot or shift
3. Final two lines (7-7) develop or respond
Description: Japanese 31-mora poem (5-7-5-7-7), longer than haiku. Used for love, lament, courtly exchange. Narrative compactness and emotional depth.
Exemplars: Manyoshu anthology; Kokin Wakashū; Saigyō; Ono no Komachi; modern English tanka writers.

## 518. Free Verse Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Open opening
2. Sequence of images and observations
3. Line-breaks shape meaning
4. Rhythmic intensification
5. Climactic image or recognition
6. Closing image
Description: Narrative poetry without fixed meter or rhyme scheme. Distinguished from prose by line-breaks and rhythmic attention; from formal verse by non-formal structure.
Exemplars: Walt Whitman's Leaves of Grass; T.S. Eliot's The Waste Land; Ezra Pound's Cantos; Allen Ginsberg's Howl; Adrienne Rich's Diving into the Wreck.

## 519. Prose Poem
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Prose paragraph opens with poetic compression
2. Image or situation rendered
3. Density of association builds
4. Climactic moment
5. Closing turn or image
6. Form remains paragraph-shaped
Description: Composition that uses prose-paragraph form but operates with poetic intensity, compression, and imagistic logic. Lyric register; paragraph structure.
Exemplars: Charles Baudelaire's Paris Spleen; Arthur Rimbaud's Illuminations; Russell Edson; Anne Carson; Claudia Rankine's Citizen.

## 520. Concrete / Visual Poem
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Visual arrangement establishes form
2. Words and shape together create meaning
3. Reading-as-seeing
4. Climactic visual moment
5. Closure through visual completion
Description: Poem in which the visual arrangement of words on the page is essential to meaning. Typographic content.
Exemplars: Eugen Gomringer's constellations; bp Nichol; Kenneth Patchen; Apollinaire's Calligrammes; e.e. cummings.

## 521. Confessional Poem
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Personal voice opens
2. Painful biographical material introduced
3. Detail accumulates without distancing
4. Emotional climax through honesty
5. No comforting resolution
6. Disclosure as the form
Description: Mid-20th-c. poetic mode of intimate first-person disclosure — mental illness, family dysfunction, trauma. Personal exposure as the poem's content and ethic.
Exemplars: Robert Lowell's Life Studies; Sylvia Plath's Ariel; Anne Sexton; W.D. Snodgrass.

## 522. Spoken Word / Slam Poetry
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Performer's voice and stance established
2. Subject (often political-personal) named
3. Rhythmic intensification
4. Wordplay and turns
5. Climactic delivery
6. Powerful close
Description: Poetry composed for live oral performance, often in competitive slam contexts. Performance-orientation, voice, rhythm, political register.
Exemplars: Saul Williams; Patricia Smith; Sarah Kay; Nikki Giovanni (proto); modern slam poetry generally.

## 523. Dub Poetry
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Reggae rhythm or musical backing
2. Performer's voice over rhythm
3. Social-political content
4. Repetition and rhythmic intensification
5. Climactic call
6. Continuation as activism
Description: Performance poetry rooted in Jamaican reggae rhythms and political content — often anti-colonial, post-colonial, anti-racist. Specific musical foundation.
Exemplars: Linton Kwesi Johnson; Mutabaruka; Benjamin Zephaniah; Jean "Binta" Breeze.

## 524. Aphorism / Maxim
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Compressed thought
2. Surprising or paradoxical structure
3. Stands alone
Description: Extremely compressed prose statement of insight or wisdom — often a single sentence. Compression and freestanding rhetorical force.
Exemplars: La Rochefoucauld's Maxims; Pascal's Pensées; Lichtenberg; Nietzsche's Beyond Good and Evil; Cioran.

## 525. Chōka (Japanese long poem)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Opening invocation or scene
2. Alternating 5-7 lines build narrative
3. Emotional intensification
4. Climactic stanza
5. Closing 7-7 couplet
Description: Classical Japanese long poem alternating 5- and 7-syllable lines, ending with two 7-syllable lines. Used for elegy, praise, narrative.
Exemplars: Manyoshu anthology; Kakinomoto no Hitomaro; Yamanoue no Okura.

## 526. Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Frame moment or theme established
2. Backstory contextualized
3. Specific scenes from life
4. Insight emerging from reflection
5. Climactic recognition or pivot
6. Reflection on what the life means now
Description: First-person account of selected periods or themes from the author's life. Distinguished from autobiography by thematic rather than chronological focus.
Exemplars: Mary Karr's The Liars' Club; Tara Westover's Educated; Michelle Obama's Becoming; Maya Angelou's I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings.

## 527. Autobiography (formal)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Birth and family context
2. Childhood
3. Education and formation
4. Career or vocation
5. Major life events and decisions
6. Late-life reflection
Description: Comprehensive first-person account of one's life — typically chronological, more formal than memoir, designed for posterity.
Exemplars: Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography; Frederick Douglass's Narrative; Malcolm X (with Haley); Obama's Dreams from My Father.

## 528. Biography
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Subject's birth and origins
2. Formation and early career
3. Major works/achievements
4. Personal life and conflicts
5. Late achievement or decline
6. Legacy and historical placement
Description: Long-form third-person account of another's life. Distinct from autobiography by its outside-perspective and from hagiography by its critical-analytical register.
Exemplars: Robert Caro's The Years of Lyndon Johnson; Walter Isaacson's biographies; Stacy Schiff; Edmund Morris.

## 529. Ethnography
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Researcher's entry into community
2. Initial observations
3. Deepening immersion
4. Cultural patterns identified
5. Specific dramatic events
6. Departure with theoretical synthesis
Description: Long-form descriptive account of a culture or community based on direct fieldwork — anthropological, sociological, journalistic. Participant-observer methodology.
Exemplars: Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific; Clifford Geertz's The Interpretation of Cultures; Adrian LeBlanc's Random Family.

## 530. Travelogue
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Departure or arrival in destination
2. Sense of place established
3. Encounters with people
4. Cultural insights
5. Personal transformation through travel
6. Departure with new understanding
Description: Narrative account of a journey — geographically grounded, often combining observation, encounter, and reflection. Secular orientation.
Exemplars: Bruce Chatwin's In Patagonia; Paul Theroux's travel books; Bill Bryson; Pico Iyer; Robert Macfarlane.

## 531. Nature Writing
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Specific natural setting
2. Close observation begins
3. Detail accumulates
4. Pattern or larger truth perceived
5. Climactic encounter
6. Reflection that ties nature to broader meaning
Description: Literary nonfiction grounded in close attention to the natural world — ecology, place, plants, animals, weather. Specific environmental focus and lyric register.
Exemplars: Henry David Thoreau's Walden; Annie Dillard's Pilgrim at Tinker Creek; Aldo Leopold's A Sand County Almanac; Helen Macdonald's H Is for Hawk; Robert Macfarlane.

## 532. Food Writing
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Food or meal introduced
2. Personal/cultural context
3. Detailed sensory rendering
4. People associated with the food
5. Climactic moment of taste or memory
6. Reflection on what food means
Description: Writing about food — its history, preparation, eating, social significance. Spans memoir, journalism, criticism, recipe-narrative.
Exemplars: M.F.K. Fisher's The Art of Eating; Anthony Bourdain's Kitchen Confidential; Ruth Reichl's memoirs; Michael Pollan's books; Calvin Trillin.

## 533. Sports Writing (long-form)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Sport or athlete framed with significance
2. Backstory of player or moment
3. Detailed game or career
4. Pivotal moment unpacked
5. Climactic event
6. Reflection on what sport reveals
Description: Literary nonfiction about sports — players, games, the meaning of athletic competition. Literary scope and character depth.
Exemplars: David Halberstam's The Breaks of the Game; Roger Angell on baseball; W.C. Heinz; Friday Night Lights (Bissinger); Gary Smith.

## 534. Music Criticism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Performance or recording introduced
2. Musical detail described
3. Comparison to broader tradition
4. Aesthetic judgment formed
5. Climactic claim about the work
6. Place in larger conversation
Description: Critical writing about music — performances, recordings, traditions. Specific aesthetic analysis register.
Exemplars: Greil Marcus; Ellen Willis; Nat Hentoff; Robert Christgau; Pauline Kael's stylistic kin in music.

## 535. Film Criticism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Film or filmmaker introduced
2. Synopsis (or refusal of)
3. Specific scene analyzed in detail
4. Thematic or formal claim
5. Comparative judgment
6. Closing aesthetic claim
Description: Critical writing about cinema — individual films, directors, movements. Specific cinematic-aesthetic analysis.
Exemplars: Pauline Kael; Andrew Sarris; Manny Farber; Jonathan Rosenbaum; J. Hoberman; Anthony Lane; Amy Taubin.

## 536. Art Criticism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Artwork or exhibition introduced
2. Visual description and detail
3. Art-historical placement
4. Aesthetic judgment
5. Climactic claim
6. Implications for the artist or moment
Description: Critical writing about visual art — exhibitions, artists, movements. Specific visual-aesthetic register.
Exemplars: Clement Greenberg; Susan Sontag; John Berger's Ways of Seeing; Jerry Saltz; Roberta Smith; Hilton Als.

## 537. Cultural Criticism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cultural object or phenomenon introduced
2. Specifics rendered
3. Critical framework applied
4. Argument advanced
5. Implications drawn for broader culture
6. Closing position
Description: Essays analyzing cultural phenomena — TV, movements, technology, public figures, political moments. Combines literary register with cultural-political analysis.
Exemplars: Susan Sontag's essays; Joan Didion's cultural pieces; Zadie Smith's essays; Hilton Als; Ta-Nehisi Coates; Jia Tolentino.

## 538. Literary Criticism (essay)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Writer or work introduced
2. Specific passages analyzed
3. Critical framework applied
4. Comparison to other works
5. Climactic argument
6. Place in literary tradition
Description: Essay analyzing a literary work or writer. Distinct from academic criticism by being shorter, more accessible, more author-voiced.
Exemplars: James Wood's How Fiction Works and reviews; Susan Sontag; Edmund Wilson; Cynthia Ozick; Adam Gopnik (variant).

## 539. Personal Essay
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Personal experience or observation
2. Specific scene or moment
3. Reflection unpacks the moment
4. Broader implication or claim
5. Climactic insight
6. Closing return to the personal
Description: Short to medium-length essay using first-person experience as the engine for broader reflection. Essayistic compression; personal grounding.
Exemplars: Montaigne's Essais (foundational); Joan Didion's essays; James Baldwin's essays; Annie Dillard; Phillip Lopate.

## 540. Lyric Essay
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lyric image or fragment opens
2. Associative movement rather than linear argument
3. Image-thinking
4. Climactic compression
5. Refusal of clean conclusion
6. Closing image
Description: Hybrid form combining essay's argumentative shape with poetry's compression, image, and rhythm. Greater formal experimentation than personal essay.
Exemplars: Anne Carson's The Glass Essay; Maggie Nelson's Bluets and The Argonauts; Eula Biss; Brian Doyle.

## 541. Hermit Crab Essay
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Borrowed form announced (e.g., "Field Guide to...")
2. Personal content fitted to borrowed shape
3. Tension between form and content creates resonance
4. Climactic moment within the borrowed form
5. Form's limits expose meaning
6. Closing within the form
Description: Essay that takes the form of another, non-essay genre — instruction manual, recipe, FAQ, list, syllabus — and uses that borrowed form for personal essay content.
Exemplars: Brenda Miller and Suzanne Paola's writings; Jenny Boully's The Body; many contemporary creative-nonfiction pieces.

## 542. Investigative Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Personal mystery or family question
2. Memoir frame established
3. Investigation begins
4. Documents, interviews, archives
5. Climactic discovery
6. Reckoning with what was found
Description: Hybrid of memoir and investigative reporting — author investigates their own family history, an unresolved death, a personal mystery. Dual personal-and-reportorial register.
Exemplars: Patrick Radden Keefe's Say Nothing (variant); Alison Bechdel's Fun Home; Daniel Mendelsohn's The Lost; Sarah Polley's Stories We Tell.

## 543. Trauma Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-trauma life
2. Onset of trauma
3. Worst period
4. First moment of agency or hope
5. Climactic escape or healing
6. Reflection on what survival means
Description: Memoir centered on survival of trauma — abuse, addiction, illness, war, escape. Specific extreme experience as content.
Exemplars: Cheryl Strayed's Wild; Mary Karr's The Liars' Club; Tara Westover's Educated; Hope Jahren's Lab Girl.

## 544. Spiritual / Recovery Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Pre-spiritual / pre-recovery life
2. Bottom or crisis
3. Awakening or first step
4. Spiritual practice or program
5. Climactic transformation
6. Continuing practice as life
Description: Memoir centered on the author's spiritual journey or recovery from addiction. Specific religious-recovery register and structure.
Exemplars: Anne Lamott's Traveling Mercies; Mary Karr's Lit; Caroline Knapp's Drinking: A Love Story; Augusten Burroughs's Dry; Twelve-step recovery memoirs.

## 545. Food Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Food memory or meal
2. Family and cultural context
3. Cooking process or eating ritual
4. Personal life events tied to food
5. Climactic meal or recipe
6. Recipe shared as gift
Description: Memoir whose primary frame is food — meals, cooking, family recipes, restaurant work. Food as structural through-line.
Exemplars: Ruth Reichl's memoirs; M.F.K. Fisher; Anthony Bourdain's Kitchen Confidential; Madhur Jaffrey; Heat (Buford).

## 546. Public Intellectual Essay
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Topic of public significance introduced
2. Author's authority/perspective established
3. Detailed argument with examples
4. Engagement with counterpositions
5. Climactic claim
6. Implications for public life
Description: Long-form essay by a public intellectual — combining argument, scholarship, and broad cultural-political address. Expert-voice authority and ambition for general audience.
Exemplars: Hannah Arendt's essays; James Baldwin's longer essays; Susan Sontag; Edward Said; Cornel West; Ta-Nehisi Coates.

## 547. Polemic
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Position stated forcefully
2. Antagonist position named
3. Sustained refutation
4. Positive case advanced with intensity
5. Climactic confrontation
6. Call to action or commitment
Description: Strong, often combative argumentative essay or book. Passionate, confrontational rhetorical register and explicit advocacy.
Exemplars: Christopher Hitchens's polemics; Tom Paine's Common Sense; Frederick Douglass; bell hooks's polemic essays; Andrea Dworkin.

## 548. Diary / Journal (literary)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Date and entry
2. Day's events or thoughts
3. Voice consistent across entries
4. Patterns emerge over time
5. Major life events recorded as they happen
6. Reader watches life unfold without retrospect
Description: Day-by-day or entry-by-entry first-person record kept across time, sometimes published. Real-time, accumulative structure rather than retrospective shaping.
Exemplars: Samuel Pepys's Diary; Anne Frank's Diary; Susan Sontag's journals; Virginia Woolf's diaries; Joan Didion's notebooks.

## 549. Letter (literary, public)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Salutation to specific addressee
2. Direct, intimate register
3. Specific subject taken up
4. Personal disclosure as part of argument
5. Climactic moment of address
6. Closing
Description: Open or published letter as literary form — addressed to a specific recipient but designed for public reading. Rhetorical-public ambition.
Exemplars: Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from a Birmingham Jail"; James Baldwin's "Letter to My Nephew"; Ta-Nehisi Coates's Between the World and Me.

## 550. Annotated Edition / Marginalia
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Primary text presented
2. Annotations begin to comment
3. Annotations build their own argument
4. Tension between text and annotation
5. Annotation's voice climaxes
6. Reader synthesizes text and commentary
Description: Critical apparatus — extensive footnotes or commentary — that creates a parallel narrative alongside a primary text. Dialogue-with-text register.
Exemplars: Vladimir Nabokov's Pale Fire (fictionalized variant); Pope's The Dunciad Variorum; Talmudic commentary; David Foster Wallace's annotated essays.

## 551. Wire Report
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Lead with most critical fact
2. Source or location
3. Brief context
4. Closing factual detail
Description: Brief, fact-focused dispatch from a wire service designed for syndication — fastest hard news. Extreme brevity and absolute factual register.
Exemplars: AP and Reuters wire service reports; AFP dispatches; Bloomberg flash reports.

## 552. Beat Reporting
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Topic or institution covered
2. Sources cultivated over time
3. Specific story emerges from beat-knowledge
4. Insider context provided
5. Story advances ongoing narrative
6. Closing places story in beat-context
Description: Sustained coverage of a specific topic-area (politics, courts, education) by a single reporter who develops sources and expertise. Topic-depth.
Exemplars: Beat reporters at major newspapers; Politico's beats; specific political reporters (Maggie Haberman).

## 553. Enterprise Journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Reporter notices anomaly or pattern
2. Initial reporting confirms there's a story
3. Sustained investigation
4. Sources and documents accumulated
5. Pattern revealed
6. Story published with original framing
Description: Original, self-initiated journalism that goes beyond breaking-news coverage — typically discovering or framing a story not on the daily agenda. Initiative-driven.
Exemplars: ProPublica investigations; NYT enterprise pieces; investigative team work at major outlets.

## 554. Solutions Journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Problem named with specificity
2. Solution or intervention introduced
3. How it works in practice
4. Evidence of effectiveness
5. Limitations and unanswered questions
6. Implications and replicability
Description: Reporting that focuses on responses to social problems — what's working, what's being tried, with rigorous examination. Investigative rigor and attention to evidence.
Exemplars: Solutions Journalism Network archive; Christian Science Monitor's solution-oriented reporting; YES! Magazine; NYT "Fixes" column.

## 555. Hyperlocal Journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Local situation framed
2. Neighborhood-specific context
3. Local people named and quoted
4. Specific local stakes
5. Resolution at hyperlocal scale
6. Implications for the community
Description: Reporting at the level of a single neighborhood, community, or small town — covering local politics, schools, businesses, characters that wouldn't make regional or national news.
Exemplars: Patch.com (early years); West Seattle Blog; Curbed; local weekly papers generally.

## 556. Data Journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Dataset or data question introduced
2. Methodology explained
3. Findings presented (often with visualization)
4. Pattern revealed
5. Implications drawn
6. Story humanized through specific cases
Description: Reporting in which data analysis IS the journalism — patterns in datasets reveal stories that would not be apparent through interview-based reporting.
Exemplars: Nate Silver's election work; NYT's "The Upshot"; FiveThirtyEight; ProPublica's data investigations.

## 557. Constructive Journalism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Story tied to civic significance
2. Multiple perspectives included
3. Context for understanding
4. Pathways for engagement or action
5. Resources or next steps
6. Audience addressed as participants
Description: Reporting that explicitly aims at productive outcomes — engaged democracy, problem-solving, audience empowerment — rather than just informing or critiquing.
Exemplars: Solutions Journalism Network; YES! Magazine; some BBC and NPR civic series.

## 558. Slow Journalism / Long-Read
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Patient observation begins
2. Sources cultivated over time
