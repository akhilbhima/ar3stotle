# Structures 301-400

## 301. Alien Invasion narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. First sign of alien presence
2. Initial human response (often inadequate)
3. Alien attack scales
4. Humanity rallies
5. Crucial weakness or strategy discovered
6. Survival, victory, or defeat
Description: SF in which Earth is attacked by extraterrestrial forces; humanity must resist, survive, or comprehend the invader. Distinguished from First Contact by its hostile-encounter premise.
Exemplars: H.G. Wells's The War of the Worlds; Independence Day; War of the Worlds (Spielberg); V; Edge of Tomorrow.

## 302. Pandemic / Outbreak narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Patient zero or anomaly emerges
2. Spread accelerates
3. Investigation by scientists
4. Containment fails or succeeds partially
5. Treatment or vaccine pursuit
6. Resolution at population scale, with reckoning
Description: Narrative of a disease outbreak — its origin, spread, response, and consequences. Spans medical thriller, science fiction, and post-apocalyptic. Biology against civilization.
Exemplars: Crichton's The Andromeda Strain; Preston's The Hot Zone; Contagion (2011); King's The Stand; Mandel's Station Eleven.

## 303. Dystopian YA
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Dystopian world introduced
2. Teen protagonist's family or community threatened
3. Reluctant entry into resistance
4. Romance with rebel or insider
5. Climactic confrontation with system
6. Partial victory; trilogy continues
Description: Young-adult fiction set in dystopian futures, with adolescent protagonists. YA conventions: chosen-one, romantic triangle, clear good-and-evil.
Exemplars: Collins's The Hunger Games; Roth's Divergent; Lowry's The Giver; Dashner's The Maze Runner.

## 304. Robinsonade (castaway narrative)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Disaster strands protagonist
2. Initial survival
3. Construction of shelter, tools, food supply
4. Encounter with environment or other people
5. Sustained existence
6. Rescue or final acceptance of the island
Description: Narrative of person stranded in isolation — typically on an island — who must survive and create order from scratch. Civilization-building from nothing.
Exemplars: Defoe's Robinson Crusoe; Verne's The Mysterious Island; Cast Away; Andy Weir's The Martian; Lord of the Flies.

## 305. Lost World narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Rumor of hidden world
2. Expedition assembled
3. Difficult journey to the location
4. Discovery of preserved ecosystem
5. Encounter with its creatures or peoples
6. Return — sometimes with souvenir or warning
Description: Adventure fiction in which an isolated location preserves an ancient or otherwise lost ecosystem — dinosaurs, prehistoric peoples, forgotten civilizations.
Exemplars: Doyle's The Lost World; Haggard's She; Burroughs's The Land That Time Forgot; Jurassic Park; King Kong.

## 306. Dieselpunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Alt-1930s/40s world established
2. Diesel-tech marvel introduced
3. Protagonist with hardboiled register
4. War, crime, or political intrigue tied to the tech
5. Climactic action sequence
6. Resolution with art-deco-noir aesthetic
Description: Speculative fiction set in alternate 1920s-1950s timelines with diesel-era technology aesthetics — propeller-driven aircraft, art deco, two-stroke industrial.
Exemplars: Sky Captain and the World of Tomorrow; The Iron Dream (Spinrad); BioShock aesthetic.

## 307. Slipstream / Weird Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent realist scene
2. First subtle wrongness
3. Reality's frame quietly cracks
4. The strange persists without explanation
5. Resolution declines to resolve
6. Reader left in suspended unease
Description: Fiction that exists between genres — neither fully realist, fully fantastic, nor fully horror — using estrangement and dream-logic. Refusal of clean genre placement.
Exemplars: Kelly Link's stories; Karen Russell; George Saunders; Aimee Bender; Kafka (proto-slipstream).

## 308. New Weird
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Decayed urban or biological setting
2. Protagonist with unusual skill or stigma
3. Encounter with deeply strange phenomenon
4. Investigation or pursuit
5. Confrontation with bodily or political horror
6. Transformation rather than resolution
Description: Late-20th/early-21st-c. literary movement combining weird fiction, fantasy, horror, SF — often urban-decay settings, biological-grotesque imagery, political weight.
Exemplars: China Miéville's Perdido Street Station; Jeff VanderMeer's Annihilation; M. John Harrison's Light; The Etched City (Bishop).

## 309. Hopepunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Grim or oppressive world
2. Protagonist commits a small kind act
3. Compounding kindnesses build a small space
4. Threat from the larger system
5. Defense of the kind space
6. Survival of the kindness, even partial
Description: Reaction against grimdark — fiction insisting that hope, kindness, and small acts of decency are forms of rebellion against darkness. Defiance through goodness.
Exemplars: Becky Chambers's Wayfarers series; Ted Lasso; The Good Place; Cory Doctorow's Walkaway.

## 310. Generation-Skip Future History
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Founding moment or origin
2. Each successive era's defining event
3. Civilization's rise and challenges
4. Crisis at peak
5. Decline or transformation
6. Closing perspective from far future
Description: SF that traces a civilization across centuries, jumping between generations — future-history form. Planetary or interstellar civilization, not single ship.
Exemplars: Olaf Stapledon's Last and First Men; Asimov's Foundation; Robinson's Mars trilogy; Vinge's A Deepness in the Sky.

## 311. Anthropological SF
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Anthropologist arrives at unfamiliar society
2. Initial misperception
3. Gradual learning of customs
4. Crisis that reveals deeper structure
5. Insight about what makes the society work
6. Departure, transformed
Description: SF whose primary interest is the imagined social structure, language, and cosmology of a fictional society — often through visitor-anthropologist protagonist.
Exemplars: Le Guin's The Left Hand of Darkness, The Dispossessed; Sheri Tepper; Mary Doria Russell's The Sparrow.

## 312. Hard Cyberpunk / Post-cyberpunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Networked society established with sociological depth
2. Protagonist embedded in system
3. Anomaly or system-critical event
4. Investigation through technical and political means
5. Confrontation with power-holders
6. Outcome at scale (not just personal)
Description: Cyberpunk that takes the sociology and politics of network society as central — less neon-noir, more institutional analysis. Surveillance, capital, labor.
Exemplars: Stephenson's The Diamond Age and Cryptonomicon; Doctorow's Little Brother and Walkaway; Gibson's Pattern Recognition.

## 313. Wuxia-Influenced Fantasy (Western adaptation)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Martial-arts world introduced (Western-fantasy variant)
2. Sworn brotherhood or master-pupil
3. Energy/power cultivation
4. Honor code conflict
5. Climactic duel
6. Resolution with retreat into solitude
Description: Western fantasy drawing structural elements from Chinese wuxia — martial-honor codes, qi-energy systems, jianghu-like underworlds. Distinct from native wuxia by being non-Chinese-cultural.
Exemplars: Fonda Lee's Jade City; Saladin Ahmed's Throne of the Crescent Moon.

## 314. LitRPG (Literary Role-Playing Game)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist enters game-world or wakes with game-rules
2. First level-up or class choice
3. Quest given
4. Skill grinding and stat optimization
5. Boss encounter
6. Level achieved; new tier opens
Description: Speculative fiction that incorporates explicit RPG mechanics — leveling up, stats, classes — into the narrative. Diegetic game-system.
Exemplars: Dungeon Crawler Carl (Dinniman); He Who Fights with Monsters; Andrew Rowe's Arcane Ascension; Russian Play to Live series.

## 315. Police Procedural
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crime occurs and is reported
2. Initial response and crime scene
3. Methodical evidence-gathering
4. Witness interviews and dead ends
5. Breakthrough through procedure
6. Arrest and case closure
Description: Crime narrative following methodical investigation by police — emphasizing routines, hierarchies, and frustrations of professional detective work. Institutional, multi-actor focus.
Exemplars: Hill Street Blues; Law & Order; The Wire; Ed McBain's 87th Precinct novels.

## 316. Locked Room Mystery
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Impossible crime discovered
2. Detective verifies impossibility
3. False solutions explored
4. Method's clue surfaces
5. Mechanism explained
6. Culprit revealed
Description: Mystery in which a crime appears physically impossible — committed in a sealed environment with no apparent way for the perpetrator to enter or exit. The puzzle is the mechanism.
Exemplars: Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue"; John Dickson Carr's The Hollow Man; Christie's And Then There Were None; Knives Out.

## 317. Howcatchem (Inverted Detective Story)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crime committed — audience watches
2. Criminal's apparent escape
3. Detective enters and notices something
4. Pursuit through evidence
5. Final confrontation with the criminal trapped
Description: Inverted from whodunit — audience knows from beginning who committed the crime, and the pleasure is watching the detective methodically catch the criminal.
Exemplars: Columbo; R. Austin Freeman's Dr. Thorndyke stories; Highsmith's Ripley novels (variant).

## 318. Forensic Procedural
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Body or evidence found
2. Crime scene processed
3. Forensic analysis begins
4. Each test narrows possibilities
5. Forensic insight cracks the case
6. Resolution
Description: Crime narrative centered on forensic science — DNA, ballistics, autopsy, toxicology — as the detective's primary tool. Foregrounds the laboratory.
Exemplars: CSI franchise; Bones; Patricia Cornwell's Kay Scarpetta novels; Kathy Reichs's Tempe Brennan.

## 319. Cold Case Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Old unsolved case introduced
2. New angle or evidence emerges
3. Original investigators revisited
4. Witnesses re-interviewed (often aged)
5. Truth emerges from accumulated time
6. Justice served late
Description: Old, unsolved crime is reopened — typically because of new evidence, new technology, or persistent investigators. Archaeology-of-crime register.
Exemplars: Cold Case (TV); Mindhunter; Michelle McNamara's I'll Be Gone in the Dark; true-crime podcasts.

## 320. True Crime narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crime introduced (often with horror)
2. Victim humanized
3. Investigation traced
4. Perpetrator's psychology examined
5. Justice (or its absence)
6. Lasting questions about meaning
Description: Non-fiction account of an actual crime, typically a murder, told with novelistic technique. Distinct from journalism by literary scope; from fiction by documentary grounding.
Exemplars: Capote's In Cold Blood; Ann Rule's The Stranger Beside Me; Helter Skelter (Bugliosi); Serial podcast.

## 321. Caper / Comic Heist
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Eccentric mark or target
2. Quirky team assembled
3. Plan
4. Comic complications during execution
5. Plan unfolds with twist
6. Light triumph
Description: Lighter, comedic variant of the heist. The team is more eccentric, stakes more whimsical, tone playful. Heist that entertains rather than menaces.
Exemplars: The Sting; Snatch; The Italian Job; Logan Lucky; Going in Style.

## 322. Noir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cynical protagonist drawn into a case
2. Shadowy world introduced
3. Femme fatale or seductive complication
4. Moral compromise begins
5. Web of corruption revealed
6. Tragic close — protagonist destroyed or diminished
Description: Crime narrative steeped in moral ambiguity, urban decay, fatalism, shadowed visual register. Pervasive philosophical despair — protagonist is doomed, system corrupt.
Exemplars: Double Indemnity; Out of the Past; Sunset Boulevard; James M. Cain's The Postman Always Rings Twice; Cornell Woolrich.

## 323. Scandinavian Noir (Nordic Noir)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Bleak Nordic setting
2. Disturbing crime
3. Investigator with personal damage
4. Investigation reveals social rot
5. Solution arrives at unbearable cost
6. Critique of Nordic society's hidden failures
Description: Crime fiction from Nordic countries combining bleak landscapes, social-democratic critique, morally complex investigators. Sociological depth — crime as symptom of societal disease.
Exemplars: Stieg Larsson's Millennium trilogy; Henning Mankell's Wallander; Jo Nesbø's Harry Hole; The Killing; The Bridge.

## 324. Tartan Noir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Scottish urban setting established
2. Detective with regional voice
3. Crime tied to local class or political fault line
4. Investigation through pubs and back-streets
5. Resolution with characteristic Scottish bleakness
Description: Scottish crime fiction characterized by working-class urban settings (Glasgow, Edinburgh), dark humor, political-social grit. Crime, place-specificity, and Celtic darkness.
Exemplars: Ian Rankin's Rebus; William McIlvanney's Laidlaw; Val McDermid; Denise Mina.

## 325. Conspiracy Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist stumbles into a small wrongness
2. Investigation triggers warnings
3. Friends or witnesses begin dying
4. Scope of conspiracy revealed
5. Race against deeper layers
6. Survival, exposure, or absorption
Description: Narrative in which protagonist gradually uncovers a vast hidden plot — typically governmental, corporate, or institutional — that threatens to destroy them. Paranoia-driven.
Exemplars: The Parallax View; Three Days of the Condor; Robert Ludlum novels; The Pelican Brief; The X-Files.

## 326. Political Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist embedded in political world
2. Threat to power identified
3. Investigation, often against allies
4. Stakes escalate
5. Confrontation with power-figure
6. Political resolution (sometimes pyrrhic)
Description: Thriller built around governmental power — assassination plots, electoral conspiracies, succession crises. Domestic-political setting.
Exemplars: Frederick Forsyth's The Day of the Jackal; Seven Days in May; House of Cards; Allen Drury's Advise and Consent.

## 327. Domestic Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent domestic stability
2. First crack — small lie or anomaly
3. Suspicion grows
4. Spouse/partner/family revealed as threat
5. Confrontation
6. Survival or destruction within the home
Description: Thriller set within a marriage, family, or household — the menace comes from intimate proximity. Claustrophobic setting; betrayal among supposed loved ones.
Exemplars: Gillian Flynn's Gone Girl; Paula Hawkins's The Girl on the Train; Liane Moriarty's Big Little Lies; Rebecca (du Maurier).

## 328. Psychological Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's mental fragility introduced
2. External pressure begins
3. Reality starts seeming unreliable
4. Reader's trust in narrator destabilized
5. Truth surfaces (or doesn't)
6. Resolution that recontextualizes everything
Description: Thriller in which the engine is the protagonist's psychological state — paranoia, gaslighting, unreliable perception. Threat is partly internal; reader cannot trust the viewpoint.
Exemplars: Gaslight (1944); Shutter Island; Black Swan; Patricia Highsmith's The Talented Mr. Ripley; Memento.

## 329. Serial Killer Thriller
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. First victim discovered
2. Pattern recognized
3. Investigator profiles the killer
4. New victims accelerate
5. Cat-and-mouse with killer
6. Confrontation and capture (or escape)
Description: Thriller centered on the pursuit of a serial murderer — often paired with psychologically scarred investigator who must enter the killer's mind. Ritual-of-killing patterns and forensic-psychological investigation.
Exemplars: Thomas Harris's Red Dragon and The Silence of the Lambs; Jeffery Deaver's Lincoln Rhyme; Karin Slaughter; Mindhunter; Hannibal.

## 330. Whydunit
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Crime committed; perpetrator known
2. Investigation pivots to motive
3. Backstory excavated
4. Buried wound or grievance discovered
5. Why the act became inevitable
6. Moral or psychological reckoning
Description: Mystery in which the criminal's identity is known or revealed early; the question and investigation focus on why — motive, history, psychology. Emphasis on understanding rather than capture.
Exemplars: Lionel Shriver's We Need to Talk About Kevin; modern literary thrillers; The Killing season 1; I Saw the Devil.

## 331. Locked Room Heist
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Single contained target identified
2. Reconnaissance of the space
3. Plan calibrated to the constraints
4. Entry
5. Operations within the locked space
6. Exit through tight window
Description: Heist set within a single, enclosed location — bank vault, museum, casino. Compressed setting; the team must operate within tight spatial and temporal constraints.
Exemplars: Inside Man; The Bank Job; portions of Ocean's Eleven; Money Heist (extended).

## 332. Procedural Drama (general)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Workplace established
2. New case introduced (cold open)
3. Initial approach
4. Complication
5. Resolution of episode case
6. Personal/serialized subplot advances
Description: Episodic drama centered on the routines of a profession (medical, legal, police, scientific) where each episode features a self-contained case while a serialized arc plays out across episodes.
Exemplars: Law & Order; ER; Grey's Anatomy; House M.D.; Bones; NCIS.

## 333. Medical Mystery
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Patient with mysterious symptoms
2. Initial diagnoses fail
3. Differential narrows
4. Crucial clue or test
5. Diagnosis and treatment
6. Recovery, complication, or terminal acceptance
Description: Mystery in which the puzzle is a baffling medical condition — a rare disease, an unexplained outbreak, an undiagnosed patient. The detective is a doctor; the criminal is biology.
Exemplars: House M.D.; Berton Roueché's Eleven Blue Men; Atul Gawande essays; Lisa Sanders's Diagnosis column.

## 334. Treasure Hunt
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Map, cipher, or rumor surfaces
2. Team assembled
3. Journey to first clue location
4. Each clue leads to the next
5. Rivals close in
6. Treasure reached (or proved illusory)
Description: Quest narrative organized around a hidden valuable — gold, artifact, document — guarded by clues, traps, and rivals. Cipher-puzzle mechanics.
Exemplars: Stevenson's Treasure Island; Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade; National Treasure; H. Rider Haggard's King Solomon's Mines.

## 335. Spy Procedural / Tradecraft Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Intelligence office or operation introduced
2. Mission assigned through channels
3. Tradecraft executed step by step
4. Compromise or unexpected variable
5. Resolution through bureaucratic and operational means
6. Reflection on costs
Description: Spy narrative emphasizing the bureaucratic, methodical work of intelligence services — agent runners, analysts, debriefings — over Bond-style action. Realism and institutional texture.
Exemplars: John le Carré's George Smiley novels; The Americans; Charles McCarry's Paul Christopher series.

## 336. Conspiracy of Silence narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Newcomer or returning prodigal arrives
2. Notices something not spoken about
3. Begins asking
4. Community closes ranks
5. Truth uncovered
6. Community reckons (or not) with what was hidden
Description: A small community or institution maintains a shared silence around an old wrong — death, injustice, secret — and the protagonist's investigation threatens to break the silence.
Exemplars: Tana French's In the Woods; Kate Atkinson's Jackson Brodie novels; Donna Tartt's The Secret History; Mare of Easttown.

## 337. Detective Memoir
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Detective's career frame
2. First case remembered
3. Series of cases, each a chapter
4. Cases reveal detective's evolution
5. Final case or retirement
6. Reflection on the work
Description: First-person narrative of a real or fictional detective's career, structured as remembered cases. Retrospective autobiographical voice.
Exemplars: Vincent Bugliosi's Helter Skelter; The Spy Who Loved Us (Bashford); fictional detective frame in The Maltese Falcon's narration.

## 338. Heist-Gone-Wrong
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Team assembles
2. Plan made
3. Execution begins
4. First serious deviation
5. Cascade of failures
6. Tragic outcome — death, betrayal, or capture
Description: Heist narrative whose pleasure is the failure of the plan — at some point the elaborate setup unravels, and the rest of the story is the consequences.
Exemplars: Reservoir Dogs; Dog Day Afternoon; Killing Them Softly; the Coen Brothers' Fargo (variant).

## 339. Crime of Passion narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Relationship and emotional pressure built
2. Triggering event
3. Sudden act
4. Aftermath, attempt to flee or hide
5. Capture or confession
6. Moral and legal reckoning
Description: Crime committed in sudden emotional storm — jealous love, betrayed loyalty, witnessed cruelty. Unplanned, single-event character; the genre's question is moral attribution.
Exemplars: Camus's The Stranger (variant); Dreiser's An American Tragedy; Patricia Highsmith novels; In Cold Blood's perpetrator psychology.

## 340. High Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Secondary world established with depth
2. Cosmic-scale threat introduced
3. Hero from humble origins called
4. Fellowship gathered
5. Quest across the world
6. Trials and losses
7. Final confrontation
8. Restoration of the world
Description: Fantasy set in a fully invented secondary world with its own geography, history, languages, and metaphysics. Total imaginative immersion.
Exemplars: Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings; Robert Jordan's Wheel of Time; Brandon Sanderson's Stormlight Archive; Le Guin's Earthsea.

## 341. Sword and Sorcery
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Hero introduced in a specific corner of a magical world
2. Encounter with a magical threat
3. Combat with steel and wit
4. Magical confrontation
5. Hero survives and moves on
Description: Fantasy focused on a wandering hero (often a barbarian or rogue) confronting magical antagonists in self-contained adventures. Smaller scale, looser world-building.
Exemplars: Robert E. Howard's Conan stories; Fritz Leiber's Fafhrd and Gray Mouser; Michael Moorcock's Elric; Joe Abercrombie's Best Served Cold.

## 342. Portal Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist in ordinary world
2. Discovery or accidental crossing of portal
3. Magical world encountered
4. Quest in the magical world
5. Crisis whose resolution requires return
6. Crossing back, transformed
Description: Fantasy in which protagonists from the everyday world cross into a magical world through a specific portal — wardrobe, rabbit-hole, mirror. The crossing itself is structural.
Exemplars: Lewis Carroll's Alice in Wonderland; C.S. Lewis's The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe; The Wizard of Oz; Spirited Away; Neil Gaiman's Coraline.

## 343. Grimdark Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Brutal world established
2. Compromised protagonist introduced
3. Quest or war begins
4. Moral ambiguity intensifies
5. Victories at unbearable cost
6. Closing — survival, not redemption
Description: Fantasy that systematically subverts heroic conventions — protagonists morally compromised, victories pyrrhic, world brutal. Fantasy's complicity with violence brought into focus.
Exemplars: George R.R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire; Joe Abercrombie's The First Law; Mark Lawrence's Prince of Thorns; The Witcher (Sapkowski).

## 344. Urban Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Modern city established
2. Hidden supernatural world revealed
3. Protagonist navigates both
4. Magical/supernatural threat
5. Investigation through occult and mundane means
6. Resolution preserving the masquerade
Description: Fantasy set in modern urban environments where magic, supernatural creatures, or hidden worlds operate alongside ordinary life. Contemporary setting, noir-influenced register.
Exemplars: Jim Butcher's Dresden Files; Ben Aaronovitch's Rivers of London; Neil Gaiman's American Gods; Charlaine Harris's Sookie Stackhouse; Buffy the Vampire Slayer.

## 345. Romantasy (Romance + Fantasy)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Fantasy world introduced
2. Lovers meet across faction or species
3. Magical or political conflict
4. Romance and conflict intertwined
5. Climactic confrontation
6. Romance and world both resolved
Description: Hybrid combining fantasy world-building with central romantic plot. Romance gets equal or primary weight.
Exemplars: Sarah J. Maas's A Court of Thorns and Roses; Rebecca Yarros's Fourth Wing; Naomi Novik's Spinning Silver; Holly Black's Cruel Prince.

## 346. Court Intrigue Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Court and its factions established
2. Disturbance to succession or balance
3. Protagonist navigates alliances
4. Betrayals and revelations
5. Climactic court confrontation
6. New political order
Description: Fantasy centered on royal courts — succession, scheming, marriage politics, advisers' rivalries. Magic often present but secondary to political maneuvering.
Exemplars: George R.R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire; Jacqueline Carey's Kushiel novels; Katherine Addison's The Goblin Emperor; The Lions of Al-Rassan (Kay).

## 347. Dark Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Cursed or shadowed world
2. Protagonist enters or already burdened
3. Encounter with horror-fantasy threat
4. Cost paid (sanity, body, soul)
5. Confrontation
6. Survival at heavy cost
Description: Fantasy with horror's atmosphere and stakes — gothic darkness, body-horror creatures, ambiguous magic. Distinct from horror by its fantasy structure; from grimdark by supernatural-rather-than-cynical register.
Exemplars: Clive Barker's Imajica; Glen Cook's Black Company; Berserk (Miura); Susanna Clarke's Piranesi.

## 348. Fairy Tale Retelling
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Source tale signaled
2. Familiar setup with new angle
3. Departure from expected path
4. Recontextualized obstacle
5. Climactic event in conversation with source
6. Resolution that reveals what the original concealed
Description: Modern reworking of classical fairy tale — often inverting, complicating, or contemporizing. Pleasure is recognition and revision of an inherited tale.
Exemplars: Angela Carter's The Bloody Chamber; Gregory Maguire's Wicked; Robin McKinley's Beauty; Once Upon a Time (TV).

## 349. Mythopoeia
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. New cosmology introduced
2. Original gods, heroes, or first beings
3. Founding event or transgression
4. World-shaping consequences
5. Continuing presence of mythic figures
6. Mythic explanation for present reality
Description: Original myth-creation — fiction that invents new mythology rather than retelling existing ones. Mythic register and ambition.
Exemplars: Tolkien's Silmarillion; Lord Dunsany's The Gods of Pegana; Neil Gaiman's American Gods (variant); C.S. Lewis's Narnia.

## 350. Dying Earth
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Diminished, late-stage world
2. Protagonist navigating its remnants
3. Last marvels and dangers encountered
4. Search for meaning at the end
5. Climactic loss or transcendence
6. World still ends
Description: Fantasy set in a world at the very end of its time — sun dimming, magic fading, civilizations collapsed into ruin. Melancholy register and end-of-the-world atmosphere as constant.
Exemplars: Jack Vance's Dying Earth sequence; Gene Wolfe's Book of the New Sun; Clark Ashton Smith's Zothique stories; M. John Harrison's Viriconium.

## 351. Mythic Fiction
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mythic figure or motif introduced
2. Modern setting that frames it
3. Encounter that reveals myth's continuing power
4. Protagonist drawn into mythic role
5. Climactic event that fulfills or revises the myth
6. Modern world subtly altered
Description: Modern fiction that explicitly draws on mythic structures and figures, often setting them in contemporary context. More reverent treatment of myth than urban fantasy.
Exemplars: Madeline Miller's Circe and Song of Achilles; Susan Cooper's The Dark Is Rising; Charles de Lint.

## 352. Fabulism
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Fable-frame opening
2. Allegorical situation introduced
3. Talking animal or magical element
4. Moral-shaped event
5. Twist that complicates the moral
6. Closing that resists clean lesson
Description: Short fiction in the mode of fable — talking animals, allegorical figures, magical events — but composed for adult audiences with modern sensibility.
Exemplars: Italo Calvino's Cosmicomics and Invisible Cities; Jorge Luis Borges; Aimee Bender's stories; George Saunders's Fox 8.

## 353. Progression Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's starting abilities established
2. Power-system rules clarified
3. First breakthrough
4. Greater challenge requiring further growth
5. Iterative cycles of training and breakthroughs
6. Capstone tier reached
Description: Fantasy emphasizing the protagonist's continuous, measurable progression in skill, magical power, or rank — often related to Eastern xianxia/cultivation but in Western worlds.
Exemplars: Will Wight's Cradle series; Andrew Rowe's Arcane Ascension; Travis Baldree's Legends & Lattes (variant); web-fiction progression-fantasy generally.

## 354. Chivalric Romance (Medieval)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Knight introduced at court
2. Adventure or quest assigned
3. Combat with monsters or rival knights
4. Encounter with lady
5. Test of courtly love and chivalric virtue
6. Return with quest fulfilled
Description: Medieval European narrative form featuring noble knights on quests of honor, courtly love, and religious devotion. Specifically medieval-Christian context.
Exemplars: Chrétien de Troyes's Arthurian romances; Sir Gawain and the Green Knight; Le Morte d'Arthur (Malory); The Romance of Tristan.

## 355. Arthurian narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Arthurian setting at Camelot
2. Specific knight introduced or sent on quest
3. Adventure in the wilderness
4. Test of virtue, often involving Otherworldly figures
5. Return to Camelot
6. Implication for the larger fall of the Round Table
Description: Vast cycle of narratives concerning King Arthur, the Knights of the Round Table, and the Matter of Britain. Distinct sub-cycle within chivalric romance with its own conventions.
Exemplars: Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia; Chrétien's Yvain, Erec, Perceval; Le Morte d'Arthur; T.H. White's The Once and Future King.

## 356. Folk-Tale (general European)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. "Once upon a time" opening
2. Protagonist (often youngest sibling) introduced
3. Departure or task assigned
4. Encounter with magical helper or obstacle
5. Reward earned
6. "Happily ever after" close
Description: Pan-European folk-tale form: short, formulaic narrative with stock figures, magical objects, and didactic close. Oral-traditional register.
Exemplars: Brothers Grimm collections; Charles Perrault; Hans Christian Andersen; Italian Calvino's Italian Folktales; Russian Afanasyev.

## 357. Historical Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Real historical setting established with care
2. Magical element woven into the period
3. Historical figures or events recontextualized
4. Protagonist navigates both real and magical
5. Climactic event that touches both registers
6. History continues, perhaps subtly altered
Description: Fantasy set in a real historical period with magic added — Tudor England with witches, Imperial Rome with gods, Napoleonic Europe with dragons.
Exemplars: Naomi Novik's Temeraire; Susanna Clarke's Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell; Guy Gavriel Kay's historical fantasies; Outlander (Gabaldon).

## 358. Faerie / Fae narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mortal protagonist encounters Fae
2. Bargain or invitation
3. Crossing into the Faerie realm
4. Strange and binding rules of Faerie
5. Crisis requiring trickery or sacrifice
6. Return to mortal world, transformed or trapped
Description: Fantasy centered on the Fae — beautiful, dangerous, alien, bound by strange laws. Faithfulness to specific folkloric conventions (Celtic and Germanic).
Exemplars: Holly Black's The Cruel Prince; Susanna Clarke's Jonathan Strange; Catherynne M. Valente's In the Night Garden; Neil Gaiman's Stardust.

## 359. Sword and Planet
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Earthling transported to alien world
2. Initial confusion and culture shock
3. Combat skills earn respect
4. Encounter with princess or noble figure
5. Quest involving sword and creature combat
6. Becomes hero of the alien world
Description: Hybrid SF/fantasy: Earthling protagonist transported to alien planet engages in sword-and-sorcery-style adventure. Distinct from sword-and-sorcery by extraterrestrial setting.
Exemplars: Edgar Rice Burroughs's A Princess of Mars (foundational); Otis Adelbert Kline; Leigh Brackett; John Carter (film).

## 360. Wainscot Society fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mundane setting established
2. Hidden society revealed to protagonist
3. Initiation into the secret world
4. Inter-relationship of the two worlds
5. Crisis that threatens the masquerade
6. Resolution maintaining or breaking it
Description: Fantasy in which a hidden community lives within or behind the structures of mundane society — borrowers in walls, secret magic schools, hidden wizard governments. The masquerade is structural.
Exemplars: Mary Norton's The Borrowers; J.K. Rowling's Wizarding World; Men in Black; Tom Holt's novels; Terry Pratchett's The Bromeliad.

## 361. Comic Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Fantasy setup with comic angle
2. Conventional fantasy event subverted
3. Escalating absurdity
4. Genuine fantasy stakes that don't break the comedy
5. Comic climax
6. Resolution that affirms both fantasy and joke
Description: Fantasy whose primary register is humor — satirizing high fantasy conventions, playing with genre tropes, mixing absurdity into world-building. Laughter as structural payoff.
Exemplars: Terry Pratchett's Discworld; Tom Holt's novels; Christopher Moore's fantasy-comedy; The Princess Bride (Goldman).

## 362. Romantic Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Fantasy world introduced
2. Lovers meet
3. World-affecting adventure begins
4. Romance deepens through shared trial
5. Climactic threat to lovers and world
6. Resolution affirming both
Description: Older variant of romantasy: fantasy adventure in which a romance arc is central but not necessarily commercially-engineered. Less formula-driven, often deeper world-building.
Exemplars: Mercedes Lackey's Valdemar series; Anne McCaffrey's Pern; Robin Hobb's Farseer trilogy.

## 363. Quiet Fantasy / Cozy Fantasy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Magical-but-quiet setting
2. Protagonist's small ambition
3. Daily routines made gently magical
4. Small disturbance
5. Community-supported solution
6. Quiet equilibrium restored
Description: Low-stakes, contemplative fantasy. World is magical but conflicts are small: opening a coffee shop with magical animals, growing potion herbs, mending the village. Comfort rather than adventure.
Exemplars: Travis Baldree's Legends & Lattes; Becky Chambers's Monk and Robot; Heather Fawcett's Emily Wilde; Howl's Moving Castle.

## 364. Saga Fantasy (multi-generational)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Founder's generation and its triumphs
2. Children's generation: inheritance and mistakes
3. Grandchildren's generation: consequence and rediscovery
4. Family curse, prophecy, or duty across generations
5. Final generation reckons with the inheritance
6. Closure (or perpetuation) of the family line
Description: Fantasy that follows multiple generations of a family, dynasty, or line — each generation a major arc. Familial-temporal sweep.
Exemplars: Robin Hobb's Realm of the Elderlings; Le Guin's Earthsea (across decades); García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude (variant).

## 365. Body Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Body in normal state
2. Subtle wrongness or contamination
3. Visible transformation begins
4. Identity threatened by the body's change
5. Climax of bodily violation
6. New permanent state — or destruction
Description: Horror grounded in the violation, transformation, or grotesque alteration of the body. Visceral, embodied focus. What the body can become.
Exemplars: David Cronenberg's films (The Fly, Videodrome); Junji Ito's manga; Annihilation (VanderMeer); The Thing (Carpenter).

## 366. Slasher
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Group of victims introduced (often archetypal)
2. Killer's backstory or signature
3. Isolated location
4. First kill
5. Successive kills with rising stakes
6. Final Girl confrontation
7. Survival or sequel-hook
Description: Horror centered on a killer (often masked, often supernatural) systematically eliminating victims — usually young people. Highly conventionalized ritual unfolding of violence.
Exemplars: Halloween; Friday the 13th; A Nightmare on Elm Street; Scream (meta-slasher).

## 367. Found Footage Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Frame: footage discovered
2. Beginning of the recording — normal life
3. Strange events captured on camera
4. Camera-people increasingly endangered
5. Climactic terror caught
6. Recording cuts out
Description: Horror presented as if assembled from real recordings discovered after the events — handheld camera, security tapes, smartphone video. Documentary-fictional structure.
Exemplars: The Blair Witch Project; Paranormal Activity; REC; Cloverfield; Host (2020).

## 368. Folk Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Outsider arrives in remote rural community
2. Community's strange customs observed
3. Outsider drawn into local rituals
4. Realization of the cult's true nature
5. Sacrifice or transformation
Description: Horror rooted in landscape, isolated communities, and ancient pagan or folk practice. Rural and pre-Christian register; terror from older orders the present has not erased.
Exemplars: The Wicker Man (1973); Midsommar; The Witch (Eggers); Penda's Fen.

## 369. Possession narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Subject in normal state
2. First strange behavior
3. Recognition that something has entered
4. Investigation and growing dread
5. Climactic exorcism or confrontation
6. Outcome — restored, lost, or transformed
Description: Horror in which a person's body is invaded and controlled by a malevolent supernatural entity. Spiritual rather than physical horror; loss of self to outside will.
Exemplars: The Exorcist; The Conjuring; Hereditary (in part); pan-cultural possession-narrative tradition.

## 370. Survival Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist trapped or stranded
2. Initial inventory of dwindling resources
3. First encounter with the threat
4. Strategic survival under pressure
5. Climactic moment of greatest danger
6. Survival, escape, or final defeat
Description: Horror grounded in protagonist's struggle to survive in environment systematically hostile — limited resources, lurking threats, attrition. Resource-management and endurance over confrontation.
Exemplars: Alien (Ridley Scott); The Descent; Resident Evil (game); A Quiet Place; The Mist.

## 371. Psychological Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's stable mental world
2. First disturbing perception
3. Reality begins fraying
4. Reader's trust in viewpoint destabilized
5. Confrontation with the source (real or psychological)
6. Resolution that may collapse all earlier interpretation
Description: Horror grounded in mental disturbance — paranoia, dread, dissociation, unreliable perception. Interior register; threat may be internal or ambiguous.
Exemplars: Shirley Jackson's The Haunting of Hill House; Kubrick's The Shining; Polanski's Repulsion; James's The Turn of the Screw.

## 372. Vampire Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Vampire's presence introduced (often hidden)
2. First victim
3. Investigation and growing dread
4. Vampire's nature confirmed
5. Confrontation with vampire-hunting tools
6. Vampire defeated, escapes, or seduces protagonist
Description: Horror or fantasy centered on vampires — undead bloodsuckers with their own conventions. Aversion to sunlight and crosses, transformation through bite, eternal life, predatory romance.
Exemplars: Bram Stoker's Dracula; Anne Rice's Interview with the Vampire; Let the Right One In; True Blood; Buffy the Vampire Slayer.

## 373. Werewolf / Lycanthropy Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's pre-transformation life
2. Curse, bite, or inheritance triggers first change
3. Transformation horror
4. Trying to control or hide the condition
5. Climactic full transformation, often violent
6. Resolution — cured, accepted, or dead
Description: Horror centered on humans who transform into wolves or wolf-like creatures — through curse, bite, or family inheritance. Body-transformation horror and dual-nature theme.
Exemplars: An American Werewolf in London; The Wolf Man (1941); Ginger Snaps; Patricia Briggs's Mercy Thompson novels.

## 374. Zombie Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Outbreak begins
2. First zombie encounter
3. Survivors form group
4. Resources, shelter, infighting
5. Climactic siege or breakthrough
6. Survival, transformation, or absorption
Description: Horror centered on the dead returning as mindless flesh-eaters. The zombie has become a dominant horror metaphor for various societal anxieties.
Exemplars: George A. Romero's Night of the Living Dead; The Walking Dead; Max Brooks's World War Z; 28 Days Later; Train to Busan.

## 375. Haunted House
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. New residents arrive at house
2. Initial unease ignored
3. Strange occurrences accumulate
4. House's history uncovered
5. House actively threatens or traps
6. Escape, exorcism, or surrender
Description: Horror in which the threat is the house itself — its history, its spirits, its inability to let people leave. Setting-as-antagonist structure.
Exemplars: Shirley Jackson's The Haunting of Hill House; The Amityville Horror; House of Leaves (Danielewski); The Conjuring; Hereditary.

## 376. Splatterpunk
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apparent normality
2. Sudden extreme violence
3. Survivors flee or fight
4. Escalating gore
5. Climax of bodily horror
6. Few or no survivors
Description: Extreme horror distinguished by graphic violence, gore, and transgressive content. Visceral, body-explicit register.
Exemplars: Clive Barker's Books of Blood; Edward Lee; Jack Ketchum's Off Season; Hostel; Saw franchise.

## 377. Quiet Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Restrained atmospheric setting
2. Subtle wrongness
3. Implication accumulates
4. Reader/viewer fills in dread
5. Final image that confirms but does not explain
6. Lingering disturbance
Description: Horror that operates through suggestion, atmosphere, and what is NOT shown rather than gore or shock. Restraint and psychological depth.
Exemplars: Robert Aickman's stories; Shirley Jackson generally; Picnic at Hanging Rock; M.R. James's ghost stories; The Haunting (1963).

## 378. Cult Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Outsider or new member encounters group
2. Initial seductive welcome
3. Strange practices observed
4. Realization of cult's true purpose
5. Attempted escape
6. Ritual sacrifice or absorption
Description: Horror in which the threat is an organized group — religious cult, secret society, hidden community — pursuing collective dark purpose. Human-collective threat.
Exemplars: The Wicker Man; Rosemary's Baby; Midsommar; Stephen King's Children of the Corn; The Sacrament.

## 379. Witch Narrative
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Witch suspected or revealed
2. Investigation, accusation, or encounter
3. Witch's power demonstrated
4. Climactic confrontation
5. Burning, banishing, or accommodation
6. Reflection on what witch represents
Description: Horror or fantasy centered on witches — old crones, hidden covens, accused women. Spans victim narratives (Salem-style) and supernatural-power narratives (Macbeth-style). Specifically gendered horror history.
Exemplars: Arthur Miller's The Crucible; The Witch (Eggers); Suspiria (Argento and remake); The Witches (Dahl/Roeg); Practical Magic.

## 380. Demonic Possession Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Subject's normal state
2. First disturbing change
3. Evidence of demonic presence
4. Priest or exorcist consulted
5. Climactic exorcism ritual
6. Demon defeated, draws back, or wins
Description: Horror sub-form: demonic-specific (vs ghost-possession) variants. Specifically Christian-demonological register with priest as exorcist.
Exemplars: The Exorcist; The Conjuring franchise; Paranormal Activity; The Last Exorcism.

## 381. Eco-Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Human encroachment on nature
2. First strange natural occurrence
3. Pattern emerges
4. Nature actively retaliates
5. Climactic ecological violence
6. Survival, often as cautionary witness
Description: Horror in which the threat is nature itself, often as response to human ecological transgression — forests that fight back, animals that rebel, environments that consume.
Exemplars: Daphne du Maurier's "The Birds"; The Happening (Shyamalan); Annihilation (VanderMeer/Garland); The Ruins; Old (Shyamalan).

## 382. Gothic Romance (genre fusion)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Innocent young woman arrives at gloomy estate
2. Mysterious dark master
3. Strange occurrences and family secrets
4. Romance against warning signs
5. Confrontation with the secret
6. Marriage, rescue, or tragic end
Description: Hybrid of gothic horror and romance — atmospheric mansion, dangerous Byronic love interest, family secret, bride in peril. Romance gets equal structural weight.
Exemplars: Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre; Daphne du Maurier's Rebecca; Victoria Holt; Mary Stewart; Crimson Peak (del Toro).

## 383. Sci-Fi Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. SF setting (spaceship, future colony, lab)
2. Strange phenomenon or contact
3. Investigation reveals threat
4. Threat spreads or escalates
5. Confrontation through technical and physical means
6. Survival or absorption — sometimes ambiguous
Description: Horror in science-fiction settings — alien parasites, malevolent AI, body-snatcher infiltration. Rational-scientific grounding of the horror element.
Exemplars: Alien; The Thing (Carpenter); Event Horizon; Annihilation; Invasion of the Body Snatchers.

## 384. J-Horror (modern Japanese horror)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Mundane modern setting
2. Encounter with cursed object/medium (videotape, phone, internet)
3. Investigation reveals haunting's origin
4. Female vengeful ghost figure encountered
5. Confrontation reveals trauma at its root
6. Curse continues or transforms
Description: Modern Japanese horror genre centered on technological haunting, female ghosts, slow-build psychological dread. Distinct from classical kaidan by media-and-technology setting.
Exemplars: Hideo Nakata's Ringu; Takashi Shimizu's Ju-On; Kiyoshi Kurosawa's Pulse; Dark Water.

## 385. Korean Horror (modern)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Family or community in tension
2. Supernatural intrusion or cursed object
3. Family secret or social wound revealed
4. Generational consequence intensifies
5. Climactic ritual or confrontation
6. Resolution often acknowledges that some wounds don't heal
Description: Modern Korean horror genre combining family/social horror, supernatural elements, and political subtext. Korean-specific cultural texture (Confucian family pressure, shamanic elements, han).
Exemplars: The Wailing (Na Hong-jin); Train to Busan (zombie variant); A Tale of Two Sisters; I Saw the Devil.

## 386. Slow-Burn Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Long establishment of normality
2. Subtle wrongness introduced gradually
3. Slow accumulation of unease
4. Major escalation only at midpoint
5. Climactic horror that reframes everything
6. Closing image that lingers
Description: Horror that develops dread gradually across an extended runtime — minimal jump scares, accumulated unease, climactic payoff that earns the wait. Patience as structural feature.
Exemplars: Hereditary (Aster); The Witch (Eggers); It Follows; The Babadook; A Ghost Story.

## 387. Found Document Horror (epistolary)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Document collection introduced (often by frame editor)
2. Earliest documents establish situation
3. Documents grow stranger and more disturbed
4. Final entries fragment or break off
5. Aftermath or rescuer's note
6. Reader-as-investigator left with implication
Description: Horror told through documents — letters, diaries, transcripts, computer files. Reader assembles the story from fragments.
Exemplars: Bram Stoker's Dracula (proto-form); Mark Z. Danielewski's House of Leaves; World War Z (Brooks); Carrie (King's epistolary frame).

## 388. Existential / Cosmic Dread Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Protagonist's stable framework of meaning
2. Event that exposes its arbitrariness
3. Inability to recover the framework
4. Increasing dread of meaninglessness
5. Confrontation with the void
6. Living-with the recognition (no resolution)
Description: Horror grounded in protagonist's confrontation with meaninglessness, mortality, or cosmic indifference. Philosophical-existential rather than alien-encounter.
Exemplars: Thomas Ligotti's stories; Robert Aickman; True Detective season 1 (in part); The Seventh Seal (Bergman).

## 389. Body Snatcher / Replacement Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Loved ones acting subtly wrong
2. Protagonist notices, others dismiss
3. Confirmation of replacement
4. Spread accelerates
5. Confrontation: who is real
6. Survival, replacement, or ambiguous close
Description: Horror in which familiar people are replaced by hostile imitations — pod people, doppelgangers, possessed loved ones. Paranoia-of-the-known register.
Exemplars: Jack Finney's The Body Snatchers; Invasion of the Body Snatchers; The Faculty; Get Out (race-coded variant).

## 390. Apocalyptic Horror
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Apocalyptic signs begin
2. Disbelief gives way to dread
3. Protagonist seeks meaning or salvation
4. Cosmic horror reveals itself
5. Final judgment or end-event
6. World ends; protagonist's last witness
Description: Horror tied to civilizational end — the last days, final judgment, cosmic curtain. The apocalypse itself is the horror, not just its aftermath.
Exemplars: The Mist (King); Cabin in the Woods (in part); The Seventh Sign; Knowing; many religious-horror narratives.

## 391. Slasher (procedural/franchise)
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Killer's iconic backstory invoked
2. New victim group
3. Setting that returns the killer
4. Rising kills
5. Final Girl confrontation
6. Killer survives for sequel
Description: Procedural slasher in which killer's mythology is the primary content (Freddy, Jason, Michael) and victim-cycle structure is iterated across sequels. Killer-as-franchise.
Exemplars: Halloween (Michael Myers); Friday the 13th (Jason); A Nightmare on Elm Street (Freddy); Scream (Ghostface).

## 392. Horror Comedy
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Horror situation set up with comic register
2. Genuinely scary event treated comically
3. Real danger that comedy doesn't dispel
4. Escalation in both registers
5. Climactic moment that is both terrifying and funny
6. Survival with the laugh hanging
Description: Hybrid where horror's conventions are played for laughter without abandoning real horror beats. Genuine horror stakes alongside comedy.
Exemplars: Shaun of the Dead; Tucker & Dale vs Evil; What We Do in the Shadows; Cabin in the Woods; Sam Raimi's Evil Dead (II onward).

## 393. Contemporary Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:
1. Modern protagonist's life and dissatisfaction
2. Meet-cute or chance encounter
3. Mutual attraction with realistic obstacle
4. Relationship development through dating
5. Crisis (often communication breakdown)
6. Reconciliation and committed relationship
Description: Romance set in the present day with realistic settings — workplace, dating apps, modern friendship circles. Now-context.
Exemplars: Emily Henry's novels; Jasmine Guillory; Beth O'Leary's The Flatshare; modern rom-com films.

## 394. Historical Romance

Beats:

4. Forbidden moments
5. Climactic sacrifice or scandal

Description: Romance set in a specific historical period — period's social rules and constraints integral to the romantic obstacle. Careful period detail.
Exemplars: Diana Gabaldon's Outlander; Eloisa James; Beverly Jenkins; The English Patient (variant).

## 395. Regency Romance

Beats:

3. Initial misunderstanding or class conflict

Description: Specific historical romance set in British Regency period (~1811-1820): balls, country estates, marriage market, proper-and-improper rules. Highly conventionalized.

## 396. Paranormal Romance

Beats:

4. Magical/supernatural threat external

## 397. Romantic Comedy (Rom-Com)

Beats:

3. Forced proximity
4. Comedic complications

## 398. Enemies to Lovers

Beats:

2. Forced proximity
3. First reluctant respect
4. Chemistry recognized despite conflict
5. Vulnerability shown
6. Love acknowledged; antagonism transformed
Description: Romance trope-based form: two characters begin in genuine antagonism that gradually transforms into love. Antagonism must be real, not feigned.

## 399. Friends to Lovers

## 400. Second Chance Romance
Tradition: Universal
Beats:

2. Tension and unresolved hurt
3. Flashbacks to original relationship
4. Why it ended revealed
5. Decision whether to try again
6. Mature reconciliation
Description: Romance in which former lovers reunite years later — past relationship's failure and present-day reconciliation are equally central. Carries weight of accumulated history.
Exemplars: Nicholas Sparks's The Notebook; Emily Henry's People We Meet on Vacation; Persuasion (Austen — original).
